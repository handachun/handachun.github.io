A Christian Response to the Problem of Evil and the Topics that Surround It
A Brief Response by Handa Chun




























April 4th, 2020
The Problem of Evil


​        The problem of evil asks, if there is a good and omnipotent God, how can there still exist evil that the good God does not desire? The existence of evil seems to suggest that this God must be either impotent in preventing such evil or must not be good.
​        Of course, when we say that evil exists, we arrive at this conclusion from observations of the world around us. It almost seems that one can point a finger at random and reveal the destruction of man and nature through senseless violence, disease, and natural disaster that cannot help but elicit from the mouths of those in suffering and the bystanders witnessing such horrors to gasp, "Why?". If Christians acknowledge that such evil exists and agree that such evils are detestable, how can these same Christians reconcile this premise with the idea that still God is good and that He is still omnipotent?
​        To answer such a question, I'd like to start off by examining what exactly is good in the first place, and from there, we can define what evil is, and so forth.


 What is good?


​        I believe, often, we try to define what is good using examples of its occurrences in the world, but rarely delve deeper to try to define what makes something good. We often say, "What is good is helping those in need, love, and peace in the world," yet the question remains, what is congruous at the center of these examples that makes them good? What is at the core of something that ordains its moral position as being "good"?
​        Is good perhaps what an individual determines is good for him/her? This may be a quick and easy answer in the current age of the self, but if so, I believe we start to fall down the spiraling pit of subjective relativism — which is a topic for a different paper. However, to address it within the context of this essay, I will claim that if the good differs from one individual to the other, a problem arises where the evil of one individual may overlap with the good of another. We find fault in such a claim as we have seen many tragedies in the world that we cannot in honest spirit say may still be "good" for another. Of course, if we believe this, then the problem of evil we are discussing in this paper would not be of much consequence as one could merely state that the problem of evil does not exist since the standard of evil that we prescribe for the formulation of this problem could merely be good for another group of people. This position shows that the nature of the human heart that is deceitful and sick (Jeremiah 17:9) in that the human heart cannot determine what is truly, universally good. This quick argument is not meant to be a full offense against subjective relativism or the reliance on the self for morality, but rather a discussional detour taken to show why such a method of defining good is not satisfactory. Another road must be taken.
​        If good cannot be determined subjective relativistically, the course of the journey shifts to an objective definition of the good. An objective view of the good is to view that no matter who the specific individual is, the definition of the good does not change. What is good for one individual must also be good for another. Now, one may notice that this is vastly different from the subjective relativistic view that many may find more amiable as the objective view of good leaves no room for the individual desires of a person to influence what can be considered good or bad. Whether an individual likes it or not, something that is good is good.
​        Now, if what is good is not dependent on the individual desires of people, where does it find its origin? If not from the person, the answer must originate from elsewhere than the self; an objective good cannot be derived from a consensus -- as that would merely be another form of subjective relativity, but it must come from some higher source.
​        For the Christian, God is the source of the definition of good as He is above all things, before all things, greater than all things, and the originator of all things (Psalm 25:8). What God desires is good and God only desires good (Psalm 33:5). This may push some questions to mind. Does this mean that God is beholden and bound to certain standards of good by which He must abide? This seems as if there is a standard above God that holds accountable what God must desire. If there is no standard above God, does God arbitrarily decide what he perceives as good? If so, this is a god who provides no clearer definition of good than those following a subjective relativistic model; we have no assurance that good will not change in a moment. The answer to this seemingly Euthyphrean dilemma is that not only does God determine good, but He himself is the ultimate good. God is not below a certain standard because He is the standard, and God's definition of good will never change because God, who is perfect in every way, will never change. Yet, though it is God and God only who defines good, we can also say that this good is not only good for God, but good for all of His creation because He designed creation to be sustained by His goodness — to be fueled by Himself (Nehemiah 9:6; Hebrews 1:3; Colossians 1:17; Psalm 145:16). Therefore, those who are dependent on God are those who are given the highest good, that is God Himself. 


What is evil?


​        From this notion, evil may be defined simply as all that God does not desire and as the desire for something that is not an emanation of the character of God. Evil is not a metaphysical object just as good is not merely a metaphysical object, but simply all that God hates and is not of God’s character. This we must understand not only within an isolation of one characteristic of God (which in itself is impossible as we understand God to be indivisible in His very nature), but within the entire character of God. Explicitly, one characteristic of God simply cannot be without all of the other characteristics of God (His love cannot be without His justice as His justice cannot be without His wrath, et cetera) because God, being perfectly one, has one character of which its characteristics are in perfect union with one another. What this means is that evil may also be an imitation or a disproportionate desire/practice of one of these characteristics of God that do not reflect the character of God.
Now, we can start to unravel how a good and omnipotent God still permits evil to exist.


The Fall


​        In the Christian story of the Fall, the fall of man, we hear a narrative of Adam and Eve bringing evil into the world by their sin. Sin is any transgression of God's law — that is, what God commands to do and not do, and therefore is an evil in itself — specifically, a moral evil (James 4:17). This act of sin is the first evil man commits that produces all other evils we see in this world. This original sin, the action of Adam and Eve eating from the tree of the knowledge of good and evil, is the first sin.
        Before the original sin, God gives a command: “of the tree of the knowledge of good and evil you shall not eat” (Genesis 2:17). The tree of the knowledge of good and evil is not just a metaphorical object or an abstract concept, but rather a physical creation of God. It is as real as the body from which we are reading these very words— it was a real, physical tree. We believe that the tree from which our first parents ate is real and therefore, we can also conclude that the action of Adam and Eve eating of the fruit of the said tree must be real as well.
        So, how does eating of a fruit from a tree condemn all of mankind and creation to be subject to evil? The rather forthright answer would be to say that the action of eating from the tree in itself was first, a sin incriminating Adam and his wife causing enmity between man and God, and second, the sin, now embedded in the nature of man, created enmity between man and nature (that is the rest of God’s creation) and man and man, and third, the result of the knowing of good and evil as provided by the fruit was the creation of sin nature within the individual that incriminated all who are from the line of Adam. One detail I will not further engage in this discourse is by which mechanism the knowledge of good and evil was passed to man from the partaking of a fruit for I do not know, but that mechanism is not of particular concern for us in this discussion.
        The first of the consequences of original sin is quite simple, and to the point. Adam and Eve both had the covenant of works to uphold before God. They were to (1) be fruitful and to multiply, (2) to subdue and to have dominion over all the earth, and most importantly, (3) to not eat of the tree of the knowledge of good and evil (Genesis 1:28, Genesis 2:17). This mirrors the three relationships that will be broken as the result of the sin of Adam: (1) relationship of mankind with itself, (2) relationship of mankind with nature, and (3) relationship between mankind and God (Genesis 3:14-19). The third is particularly important because of the fact that it points to man being a creation that is in the image of God, but not God. It emphasizes the Suzerain-vassal relationship of God and man, and therefore places mankind as creation that is meant to worship God. The covenant of works states that as long as man is able to keep the commandments, the laws of God, he is able to claim right standing before God — in him is righteousness that allows man to have life. Adam, by partaking of the tree, breaks this law that has been given by God. By taking from the forbidden tree, he has claimed autonomy from God and has set His own rule above that of God Himself, and the punishment for sin in His sovereign wrath is death (Romans 6:23). This death can be defined as the separation from God’s grace, love, and mercy only to be subjected to God’s justice, hatred, and wrath. In this way, when Adam sinned, he died immediately in the soul, and in the body, he was kept alive for a short while by the common grace of God available to all mankind. Yet, even this body, once disembodied from the soul that God breathed life into, will suffer the same fate of disintegration from God’s grace.
Second, we see that by breaking the third of the commands of the covenant of works that God provides to mankind, all of the relationships that depend on it break as well. We previously indicated in the section regarding “good,” that what is good for God is good for his creation as well since God is who they were designed to be sustained by. When the relationship with man and God breaks, all other relationships break as well. This is so because the man, not being in communion with God, cannot be in relationship with nature nor other men — he cannot keep the other commands of the covenant that God has required of them. Mankind was to subdue and have dominion over all of nature because it was to represent the relationship of God with His creation reflected in the relationship of man with nature. As we understand it, all of creation, including humanity, exists to glorify God, and all that glorifies God finds its origin in the originator of all good things — God. If man cannot have a proper relationship with the originator and creator, we cannot have any proper relationship with the creation.
This is why there is natural evil, or evil that occurs in nature (outside of actions of man) that affects negatively both man and nature itself. Mankind was created to subdue it and have dominion over it (Genesis 1:28), but with a broken relationship with the one through whom mankind understands what kind of relationship he is supposed to have with every other thing, this becomes impossible. Just as without God, there is no order, there is no order in nature if man cannot properly rule over nature as well. This causes natural evil to enter the world and creates the enmity between man and nature (Genesis 3:17-18). This enmity does not just affect man, but man does evil to nature as well. As nature in enmity with man does not do as the man pleases, man also harms and destroys nature — we see this as the environmental destruction man causes to the world. This attack on God’s creation is in itself also a moral evil (for we are to care for it — Exodus 23:11) through which we only flare up the wrath of God and the execution of His justice upon us even more severely.
If the relationship between man and nature is reminiscent of the relationship between God and His subordinate creation, the relationship of man to man is representative of (1) the relationship God has with Himself within the trinity, and (2) the relationship God has with a specific creation, the image of Himself — that is, mankind.
We shall not discuss in mature detail the subject of the trinity, but we will define the trinity as the divine perichoresis of three distinct persons of one essence (or substance or nature) that is God. The trinity shows the perfect union and relationship of the three persons that love each other perfectly, each respecting the role and office of each other with a singleness of will toward one desire of God in one essence and being. Particularly, within the ontological trinity, the eternal generation (note the “eternal”) of the Son as of the Father is to be glimpsed (in a dim light) in the relationship of man and man, pointed out in Genesis 2:22-23 showing the begotten nature of woman from man, man to his offspring, and therefore all men from one man, as one mankind. Furthermore, God, in special decree, created man in His image (Genesis 1:26-27), and breathed life into them (Genesis 2:7), thereby imparting a spiritual nature into them. This means that our spiritual selves are breathed from God (but we are not God) and originally created to be in one will with Himself — similar (in a dim light) as through the eternal procession of the Holy Spirit, the Spirit is in perfect will of the Father and the Son. I want to quickly clarify here that neither the Son nor the Spirit are any lesser in essence or nature than the Father — rather, they are of different persons of the same divine essence who are defined by the distinctions of the Father being unbegotten, the Son begotten, and the Spirit proceeding. Within the economic trinity, the Son is obedient to the will of the Father (John 5:30; 8:28), and the Spirit is proceeding from the Father and the Son to testify (John 15:26). However, due to the Fall, as we are no longer in the same will as God, we can no longer also represent the image of God-to-God love within our own mankind-to-mankind relationship.
Not only does the mankind-to-mankind relationship mirror the relationship of God with Himself, but also the relationship of God to man. We see this in the relationship of the Son with the Church (John 3:29) which is represented in the relationship of the man to the wife (Ephesians 5:23). Yet another, the man’s relationship to his family, specifically his offspring, is to be reminiscent of the relationship of the Father to his children that becomes available to us through adoption (Ephesians 1:5, Romans 8:14-15). By our broken relationship with God, we no longer understand and can work out a true relationship between mankind that represents the relationship of man to God. Whenever our works in the relationship with others veer from that which it is supposed to represent, we again commit a moral evil befitting of punishment.
Third, due to the original sin of Adam, there is an imputed sin and original sin nature to Adam’s progeny — all of mankind (Romans 5:12). This means that due to one sin, all of mankind suffers from a position of sin deserving of the wrath of God. It means that all have sinned because they from their very infancy are those with sin nature in them that have sinned already (Psalm 51:5). All are deserving of the wage of sin — death (Romans 6:23). The position that Adam stands in as illustrated in the first and second effects of Adam’s original sin becomes imputed upon us as well. We have committed the said sin with Adam and have therefore been declared guilty of it and have received the due consequences of it. We become separated from the goodness of God to face His wrath and fury that now we all deserve.


Original sin


        To understand original sin in a further detail, I will take a detour here in this section — this is merely to be a supplement for understanding why Adam’s original sin is inherited to us as well and how we suffer from it.
        Original sin is a sin that not only Adam committed, but with Adam (because he is our federal head — or representative), the whole of humanity committed against God. To specify a bit further and to use more nuance, we can split this sin into two terms, original sin, and imputed (or inherited) sin. Furthermore, I will clarify the term original sin as the first sin of Adam to distinguish from the sin nature that exists as a result of it which dwells in all man.
        In Romans 5:12-14, we see “all sinned” in verse 12 as having sinned in Adam, this leads for us to see that all have been called sinful because they sinned in Adam himself through his one federal head action of eating from the tree of the knowledge of good and evil. This means that through the original sin (first sin) of Adam, firstly a sin nature is created within us — that is to say, we are all completely steered towards evil and only desire evil. We are therefore born with an evil desire to only hate God, and hate all that is good in God. Now, from this original sin of Adam also came the imputation of that sin upon all mankind as the imputed sin of man. This means that all are not only born with a sin nature, but born with the guilt of the sin of Adam. We are born having committed a sin already that we had committed with Adam himself. Specifically, we are ultimately and completely depraved, lacking all good, and desiring only evil. This is why we are in need of a savior who needs to save us from death into life as we have died as such is the wages of sin.
        Now, all these hinge on the notion that through the action of one man came sin into all of humanity. Specifically speaking, the first sin of the first man belonged not only to that one man, but to all of humanity. This may now invoke some discomfort at the idea that one man may cause all to sin. Could the singular action of our first father necessitate the disfigurement of our right standing before God? Many may repudiate such an idea because they themselves were still not the perpetrators of the said crime and therefore the guilt of one cannot be on them as well. To this, we say that Adam was the federal head to all of humanity and therefore the stand-in for all mankind. This is to say, Adam stands as our representative in a covenant relationship with all the rest of humanity. We are not merely a recipient of the consequences of an action, but we were, along with Adam, perpetrators of the action.
There has never been a greater representative than Adam because he was in the most optimal of situations and opportunity to live out the covenant given to him. Not only that, but he was the first man — the exact design as we were supposed to be without sin or corruption. It is not that Adam just happened to be the first in the biological and spiritual genealogy of mankind, but rather, he was built to be the singular representative for all mankind to come. This is why his helper, his female counterpart, unlike the other creatures, came from his own flesh and bone — he was the progenitor to mankind. Adam represents us, and we committed original sin in him and with him because he was the best representative for mankind. If any of his descendants were in his place, they would have done no better, if not worse. And so, when Adam stood in the garden presented with a decision to either obey God and trust in the everlasting life guaranteed to him, or to desire an autonomy through the receiving of his own knowledge, Adam chose disobedience. 
The argument goes to further push that if we are to dismiss this federal headship argument, we have actively also refused the redemptive federal headship of Christ Himself. This, of course, is talking about the issue of double imputation wherein which the guilt and consequences of our sins are imputed onto Christ as the righteousness of Christ is imputed upon us. We can be imputed with guilt just as we have been imputed with Christ’s righteousness and our guilt onto Him. As we have been crucified with Christ on that cross, we have partaken of the fruit in the garden of Eden with Adam. Just as through Adam we have all died, we have lived through Jesus.
To add, when we, through Adam, ate from the tree of the knowledge of good and evil, we took up the knowledge of what is good and what is evil. However, we must not mistake that man did not have access to the knowledge of good and evil. That is, before we had decided to take on the usurpers’ roles of knowing good and evil ourselves, we had God for such ability to know what is good and evil as we walked in full communion with Him. All that God had made was good, and all that He prohibited and hated was evil. We need only have had faith in the good God that what He declared good was indeed good, and what He deemed evil was truly evil. Not only that, Adam had walked with God — Adam had the very standard for what was good in the midst of him. This is why Eve could still say that the “tree was good for food” for God had made all things in the garden good (as we see throughout the first chapter of Genesis). The forbidden tree was not evil — it was a good creation. It was the action of eating from it that was forbidden because man could not be the judge of good and evil as God is the definition of good and we can know good by Him only.
 What happened when we ate of the fruit of the forbidden tree? Yes, we did gain an autonomous understanding of good and evil. Yet, it only came to haunt us and condemn us — now filled with sin that Adam had committed, the autonomous knowledge of good and evil only made vast the gulf between God and man. It accused Adam and Eve in the very sin they had committed mere seconds ago. Sin had split us apart from the companionship and love of God, yet we had gained what we so desired: an autonomy in judging good and evil — only to have that very law in our hearts accuse us as our own evil thoughts tried to excuse ourselves. We were left with only a desire for evil from our derived sin nature while the autonomy we had so desired became a reminder of our death. Our hearts, though having the conscience to know enough of the law, ensnared in our sin nature, could only do evil and only excuse our actions by creating our own sense of right and wrong — ignoring the truth. The serpent was right that we had become “more like God,” we had an autonomous knowledge of God — but what is the use of such if it only condemns us?
Though Adam and Eve know even in their autonomy that they have sinned and rejected God, (Genesis 3:7, John 21:7) and therefore though they attempt to cover themselves with clothes of their own works, we see that no longer will their own work suffice as all their works are but “polluted garments” (Isaiah 64:6) soiled by iniquity. God must instead create, by His grace, a garment made by sacrifice that is fitting for the sin of Adam, hence foreshadowing He who will cloth us with His righteousness (Isaiah 61:10, 2 Corinthians 5:21). By eating from the tree, in the action in itself, we have sinned against God because we had desired to usurp God and be our own false idols, and as the action’s consequence, we twisted good and evil to fit what we desire in sin. When we ate from that tree, we had come to know good and evil, but in the action of taking from the tree, we had sinned and had become evil. 
At this point, one must remember, man was without sin — he was “very good”. So, with the free agency given from God, why was it that mankind still sinned against God? It was because even at that time, we lacked the faith required of us in the Holy one who gives us the best of things we cannot attain to ourselves. For God had promised the tree of life — an everlasting life — for Adam and Eve if they could resist the temptation to become gods for themselves. Even within the covenant of works, we see a glimpse of the New Covenant of faith that would become available to us through Christ. Though Adam and Eve knew what was good and what was evil before eating from the tree — that is, they knew God and His gifts were good, and knew that disobeying God was evil, they, in lack of faith that God would provide for them what is best and that God is good, chose in works to become their own determiners of good and evil. In this process, as a result of the action of eating from the forbidden tree, they (and therefore we) sinned against God, only to receive, not the Godhood, but fallen sin nature in the process.
Now, we, having chosen against God in Adam, are born with the imputed sin of Adam, and the sin nature that makes us to only sin against God.


Free agency and a good God


​        At this point, one might wonder: why did God give man the free agency that permitted sin to enter into mankind? One may argue that the decision of God to give free agency to man seems to be one that is faulty for an omniscient God should have known that man would sin. Either God gave free agency with malicious intent — thereby thwarting the argument of a good God, or He is not omniscient but has a mere mortal depth of knowledge.
Before we go any further, we must arrive at a consensus on what kind of free agency this is. When Christians say we have free agency, we mean not the libertarian free agency wherein we are not influenced by any outside forces in our decision making. Such idea of free agency I deem as illogical and dismissable as it seems to suggest that we are in all matters of our being isolated, uninfluenced by others. As long as we are not solipsists, it is a doctrine of free agency that is quite hard to reconcile with reality. Rather, for the Christian, free agency merely means that an individual has the ability to choose and make a decision when placed in a situation to do so. Chiefly, the Christian cares most for the moral free agency of the individual — choice to choose to do the good or the evil. Furthermore, I want to make sure to distinguish from free will as well — free will is different from free agency in that having free agency means that the individual has the ability to make a decision but having free will means that an individual can actually make any choice he/she desires (as we read above in the section about original sin, those who are still dead in sin cannot even do good, so therefore they have lost their free will). The question at hand in this essay is the former — free agency.
Though the free agency question may seem like a vast question, and a daunting one in form, it is quite readily answerable with the very contents of the question. God is omniscient, and God gave free agency to man strictly because He is a good God. That is to say, God gave free agency to man because it was good for man to have free agency. Free agency in itself was a good thing because it, like all the rest of His creation before the Fall, was supposed to reveal more about the perfect standard of good — God Himself. We were created in His image (Genesis 1:26), and part of that image that he so called “very good” (Genesis 1:31) was free agency. As we had driven in an earlier section of this essay, all that is good is good because it is derived from the very person of God Himself.
We see God’s free agency at work in all that He does. He always has a choice, but He always chooses the greatest good — He chooses Himself because He is the greatest good. When He sends a prophet to spare the sinful Nineveh (Jonah 3:10), He has the choice to not save us, but because in His very character He is loving, patient, merciful, and compassionate, He does so. When we see God work in all things, even when it seems that God is permitting evil to persist in the world including the foremost cause of it in the world — mankind, God is doing so because He is doing it to maximize the greatest good. As we had discussed, because this greatest good is Himself, He will make sure that He is glorified to the maximal degree. Furthermore, as we had discussed, this good that He maximizes will not only benefit Him, but all of His creation for we are dependent on Him, and we run on our fuel who is God. As one can see, God always has the free agency to do what He pleases, but we have the guarantee that our unchanging God will always do what is most like Himself.
This free agency was given to mankind as a gift. It was good that man had free agency to choose and make decisions. Hence, God also gave the tree of the knowledge of good and evil. God allowed for man to choose between man and God through the prohibition from the tree of the knowledge of good and evil. The Lord God did not place the forbidden tree in the garden to tempt man, but rather to give the man proper choice to either worship God or to worship man himself. Without the tree, free agency would have merely been a farce and an untestable character of mankind; therefore making man not an image of God, but just a common creation as any other animal.


The Good News


        How does the Fall of man, evil’s entrance into the world, and the separation from the very source of good allow for God’s glory — His maximization of good in His handiwork of creation? It seems quite incredible that all this evil, all these mishaps are what God considers as “going all according to plan”. Within the narrow scope of the man’s mind and the corrupted nature of our hearts, evil’s total reign would absolutely seem to be the most accurate portrayal of the world. Yet, God declares, “my thoughts are not your thoughts, neither are your ways my ways” (Isaiah 55:8) — and what a good news that is! If we, mortal creatures who are not God, could know all that God knows, and could execute all that God executes, I would consider that to be the greatest of all deterrents to putting my faith in an omnipotent, omniscient, and omnipresent God. If I could know all that God is doing and the reasons for doing so, this God would be the most invalid, inconsequential, and undeserved god. I could not trust that this “god” created the universe if such was the case! For the Christian, all of the fathomable universe (and the unfathomable) is a mere understatement and foretaste of all of God’s glory and magnificence. If my mind could hold all of this God’s magnificence, that would be far too little of a “god” for me.
        Not so with the Christian God. He is above all things, beyond all things, and is greater than all things. And so even before time began, He knew all that was to happen — the Creation, the Fall, the bringing up of Israel, the Incarnation of Christ, the Cross and the Resurrection, the Church Diaspora, and the Second Coming of Christ are all parts of the one narrative of God’s grace and love. This is the Good News — that God had a plan of redemption for His fallen people even from the start.
        This God did not keep His plan completely hidden, however. Rather He revealed all that we would need to know and the extent to which we could know through the Bible, and through His creation (Romans 1:20). By the words of the Scripture which are divinely inspired (2 Timothy 3:16-17), we know the narrative that reveals the same love within the trinity being expounded upon us through His creating us. Through the creation we see the relationship of God and man, and God within the trinity. All is given to us that we may need to know Him, and to trust in Him (Romans 1:21). For God created all of humanity and the rest of His magnificent creation, and gave us all things for the purposes of illustrating what already was within Himself.
        So, what exactly is this Good News?
        First, God created the world so that the elect Church may come out of it who would be given to Christ as His people. Even from the beginning, the creation was made, not for creation’s sake, but for the glory of God and the love of the three persons in the trinity showing their deep affections for each other. It could not be any other way, for if the creation was made for the creation, for the creation’s glory, the creation would be the object of all devotion — the creation would be God. In all the fallen nature that it has come to be, we can clearly see that no human nor any created thing can be gods as it is undeserving and full of evil that the creation itself sees as unfit. If on the other hand, the creation was merely a creation of a unitarian god (unlike our Trinitarian God), this would employ a god that is devoid of significant love. This god would have created all the world not as a gesture of love for another, but as a mere plaything — the creation in itself absolutely insignificant and having no other purpose than to live out as meaningless a trajectory of life as those defined by a nihilist. No, God the Father instead created the world in the purest love He pours out to the Son while the Spirit witnesses to it. The Father created the world to be a testament to the world of how much He loves the other two persons of the trinity; specifically he created the world to bring out of it the Church that He would give to the Son whom He loves as His bride, His nation, and His family.
        Second, God allowed for the Fall only because He had in mind a greater redemption story that would bring His people to Him (Genesis 3:15). God did not create the tree of the knowledge of good and evil just because He desired to create temptation for Adam and Eve. He created the tree for a good purpose as well. Either Adam would trust God to not eat of the tree and therefore enjoy the Garden with God forever, or Adam would eat of the tree so that God could redeem His faithless people to show that even though they were unfaithful, God was faithful. Through the very work of salvation that He showed to the world, He would redeem the Fall and would give even more than they had before the Fall. God gave free agency to man and allowed for the Fall because He knew that with it would come an even greater glory to Himself, and therefore the greatest good for His people (Isaiah 43:7).
        Third, God chose the ethnic people of Israel so that He may show the world what being in covenant with a gracious, loving, and powerful God would look like. He raised up Israel as a nation not because they were better than other nations, but because He loved them and decided to use them for His glory (Deuteronomy 7:6-8). God persisted and relented with adulterous people who turned away from their God (Hosea 4:1-3), and gave mercy to a faithless people (Jeremiah 3:12-14). All this for what? For the same promise He had given Adam and Eve — the protoevangelium — the promise that God would send an offspring who would rescue them (Genesis 3:15). For these people would fail over and over again to show that their fallen nature and their insufficient ability to work out the covenants given to Noah, Abraham, and Jacob would come to show their need for the New Covenant given by Christ’s fulfillment of the works required for eternal life (Romans 8:3-4). For from these people would come the Messiah, and they were told to hope in this Messiah who was to come. Even the Jewish heroes of old were saved by their faith in the Messiah who was to come, for they could not keep up works that would satisfy the conditions of perfection that God required (Hebrews 11:1-2).
        Fourth, the Son of God, who was God (John 1:1), became flesh to dwell among all mankind (John 1:14). He became flesh because mankind needed a savior who could take on the sins of mankind and only a man can die for another man. God debased Himself to the level of man because man could not rise to the level of God (Philippians 2:5-7). What a scandal this is! God, the highest of all beings, did not turn His face away from mankind, His disobedient creation, but instead came to earth to sympathize with mankind yet stay completely and utterly faithful to God lacking the sin and sinful nature that all mankind has (Hebrews 4:15). He was tempted in every way, one of such we see in the desert when He was tempted with power (which He had all of), with food (of which He had a better bread — John 4:32-34), and with safety (which He had give up for the sake of the Cross) by the evil one, Satan (Matthew 4:1-11; Luke 4:1-13). The fact that God would come to be born of a woman to come into the world as a babe is an idea unfathomable, and perhaps this is why the Jews of the times rejected Him (Matthew 13:57; Mark 6:4).
        Fifth, Christ died and rose again for us. He did not die like an ordinary man — He did not die accused of the sin within Him because He had never sinned, but He died undeservingly. All of mankind, as we had discussed, deserved to die because of their sin, but not so with Jesus. For He was born of a virgin, not under the federal headship of Adam (that is man), yet also He was fully a human being born of a woman (Luke 1:35). The Bible further confirms He never sinned in His life (1 Peter 2:22). Yet, we know that Jesus died — why? Christ, the God incarnate, sinless and perfect, died, not for His own sins, but for the sins of mankind (2 Corinthians 5:21). He was the Passover lamb (Exodus 12:21) slain so that we who believe would not be condemned, but have eternal life (John 3:16). When Jesus cried “eli, eli, lema sabachthani?” asking God why He had been forsaken (Matthew 27:46), yes, He was fulfilling Psalm 22, but it was not merely a reference to a popular poetry as a well-read literature professor would make. No, for Psalm 22 in itself is a Messianic prophecy — the words of David in Psalm 22 were the words inspired by the Spirit of Christ (1 Peter 1:10-11). When David cries out words of the verses 14-18 of Psalm 22, they are the distant echoes of Christ’s words on the Cross, and the throbbing of the heart of Christ from Golgotha. We see that all throughout history, God had spoken of this moment of reconciliation. Whether it is the Laws, the Psalms, the book of Esther, the prophecies of Jeremiah, or even the New Testament, one person’s image stands out clearly against the backdrop of the universe and its inhabitants — the slain Lamb of God. Such shadows as the ram in bush (Genesis 22:13-14), the bronze snake (Numbers 21:8-9), and the man in the burning furnace (Daniel 3:25) are but Christophanies that speak of the penal substitution of Christ for us that the Son of God would become cursed on a tree (Deuteronomy 21:23; Galatians 3:13). When we ask the question of the problem of evil, we cannot forget that it was us who brought it into the world, and Christ who bore the ultimate penalty.
Yet, of course, Jesus did not stay in the tomb. He arose from death to resurrection life (Luke 24:1-12). Death could not hold a sinless savior; for in a moment of death, Jesus paid the price for the lives of all the elect. Jesus overcame death with His own death, and now in His resurrection, we also have the hope of the resurrection of our own bodies (1 Corinthians 15:51-57). Christians, therefore, can hope for the resurrection of our own bodies as we know that Christ has resurrected (1 Corinthians 15:12). He is our victorious Lion of Judah who is the slain Lamb of God (Revelation 5:5-6). Through His resurrection, we have regeneration of our souls to come alive and live once again in Christ to enter into His Kingdom (John 3:3-7).
        Sixth, Christ has died and risen again to buy the Church (Acts 20:28). The Church is none other than those who are the elect whom God foreknew and prepared as Christ’s bride (Romans 8:29). They have come to know Christ to be called saints and they will be in one multitude from all the nations of the world to form one people under the one God (Revelation 7:9-10). They are not distinguishable by their physical appearance or their status, but by the regenerated nature born of water and Spirit (John 3:8). These people, bought with the blood of Christ (Acts 20:28), have gained all their treasures of knowledge and wisdom leading to faith in Christ (Colossians 2:2-3), and cannot help but worship the God who lives (Acts 4:19-20). They were people once dead in their sins in the same iniquity of Adam and their own (Ephesians 2:1-3), causing all evil in the world through the demonstrations of their evil upon evil, but in receiving grace upon grace through Christ Jesus (John 1:16), they have become the salt and the light of the world (Matthew 5:13-16). The Gospel within them shines brightly as physical works as well for their faith is explicably shown through their works (James 2:14-17). They share in all that they own physically as they do so in their faith as well — the physical actions and life of the saint is reflective of the change that has taken place within — they fellowship with one another and feed each other of their needs as if one physical body (Acts 2:42-47). As Christ had loved them and poured out His grace upon them, they reflect the love given by feeding the widows and the orphans (James 1:27), and healing the lame (Acts 3:5-6). As Christ is their solution to the evil in the world, and as they are imitators of Christ, they become part of the solution to fight evil in this world (Ephesians 5:1-2).
        Seventh, finally, for the Christian, the end to all evil and the redemptive story does not end with the Church regenerated in just the soul, and not in the physical body. As we are both soul and body, there will be a day when we will be resurrected in the body as well. When we are resurrected as was Christ resurrected, that day a new heavens and new earth will be established where all the evils we see today will be no more (Revelation 21:1-5; Isaiah 65:17-25). For the saints, for those who are renewed in Christ, this will be a great day. This will be when Christ comes once again to make all things right — this will be a time when the Son of God takes His rightful place among the saints and will rule as King forever (Revelation 22:12-14). Yet, at the same time, for those who are not deserving of the grace of God will receive the wrath of God (Revelation 21:8). Those who deserve the wrath of God is all mankind who have not received the Lord Jesus as their savior, but those who have washed their robes perfectly clean are those who have been cleansed by the blood of Jesus Christ and therefore considered righteous (Romans 5:18-21). For mankind has brought all evil into the world, they must pay the price if they will not trust Jesus to take it for them.


Conclusion


        The problem of evil is not something ignored by Christians. We do not wave our hands and ignorantly cover our eyes to the evils of this world. Rather, we recognize the root of the problem of evil which is the sin of mankind, and ask God to take care of such evil through His Son, Jesus Christ. Evil is not what God wants, yet He allows it for a short while so that greater good can be had. God, in His great mercy, had given us all we needed, yet we rejected Him to bring sin into the world. This moral evil corrupted us and through us, the rest of God’s creation. Yet, God gave us a hope through Jesus whom we have only to have faith in to be saved from all evils of the world, including ourselves. Those who have been saved can go on to not only no longer sin, but do good that will put down evil in this world.
        Looking out onto the world, it may seem hopeless and painful. Even I, writing at this time, am going through many hardships including the illness of my father, our family’s inability to do anything about it, a world pandemic, relational strife, and mental struggles. These do not label the God of our hope with inability, but rather highlight the very sinful nature of who we are. God, knowing all the pain and suffering of mankind, did not stay on His throne above, but came down to live in pain and suffering like us, die in place of us, and lived again in a new body so as to give us hope of our own resurrection as well.
        This is the God the Christians have their eternal hope in. It is not the giddy feeling of experiencing an “emotional high” in worship, nor the intellectual prowess stoking our pride, but rather the assurance and conviction (Hebrews 11:1) in a God who cares, loves and is still standing before His throne watching over us (Acts 7:55). I invite you all to know this God.
