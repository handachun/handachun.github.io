Imago Dei and Christian Charity

How the Christian Ought to View the Fellow Man and Serve Him

“So God created man in his own image, in the image of God he created him; male and female he created them.”

Genesis 1:27

In the beginning was God, and He created man (male and female) in His own image. Of all that God speaks into creation in Genesis chapters one and two, only one was created in the image of God, imago Dei. For only one of His creation was endowed with the great privilege of bearing the image of God— man. It was for this image of God that God had codified the laws of physics, made the earth, formed the celestial light, and created all plants and animals to be under man’s dominion. All of the other plants and critters of the earth and sea were created “according to their kinds”, but only man was created to be of the image of God. Only one creation enjoyed the status of reflecting not only the miscellaneous attributes and abilities of God, but the very image of God Himself.

Unfortunately, this idea of mankind being images of God is often left untaught by the Church today, and when taught, often the phrase, “image of God”, is thrown around without much clarification as to what it entails. I find such ignorance to be of a genuine problem as it shapes every sphere of how we, as images of God, ought to interact with other images of God. God made the human race to be in communion with one another from the very beginning. When God first created Adam alone, God saw that it was not good that the man was alone. This was not because God had made a mistake, but it was to show that man is unlike any other creature of the earth and hence requires another God’s image to be in communion with him. God created both Adam and Eve to show that God is also always in community with Himself. Man, the image of the divine, ought to also be in community as God is in community. From Adam, God creates the woman, Eve, to show that man is not to be alone in His proper representation of God, but is to be in community with the fellow images of God. Knowing this, even if we are fully comfortable with our own being without having to question our identity as images of God, if we are to live in community, we must be inclined to ask how we are to treat the other men if they are what we call the “images of God”.

Of course, such a question is no virgin matter. The question of what it metaphysically and theologically means for man to be an image of God has been explored by almost every respectable theologian. In this essay, I do not dare to define in perfect exactitude what an imago Dei is, but I do hope to convey, in a concise enough manner, a frame of reference or understanding that may be helpful in our approach to matters of relation between those of mankind. I certainly do not wish to venture into a debate on the matter, or to contradict any of the foundational theologians who are far more knowledgeable and wise on the matter than I. I merely seek to create a humble metaphorical understanding that may help the reader to grasp the concept of imago Dei with applicable conceptualization that may come to be of aid when the reader may need to exert himself in an analysis on matters of Godly worship or human relations. Hence, I will focus largely on the relational aspect of imago Dei rather than the functional aspect of it.



Improper Views of Imago Dei

I will start off with an explanation of what we must reject as improper definitions of the Christian idea of the image of God. First, the designation of man as an image of God most certainly does not mean that man is of some part of God that has been allotted to the personhood of man. This idea is derived from a misinterpretation of God’s breathing life into man (Genesis 2:7) as some form of God’s Spirit coming to become a part of man. Considering the fact that mankind has revolted against God, this is an absurd interpretation which would render God as either susceptible to act against His own nature, or as divisible from His original nature. We know, firstly, that God cannot act against Himself as He is without contradiction and is in perfect unity within Himself. Neither can God be divided from His own original nature because such a claim would indicate that God can come apart from His perfect union with Himself which would be contrary to the doctrine of the simplicity of God.

Second, the idea that somehow the designation of man as an image of God means that he is a deity must also be dismissed. This is an absurdity first and foremost because God makes it known throughout the Scriptures that He is the only God, and He cannot be considered to be if He has a separate deity that competes for His position. If this simple contradiction is not enough, let us clarify what would be required if the image of God were to mean that the image bearer held the fullness of deity. By deity, if he is modeled after Yahweh and is deserving of respect in the same sense as of Yahweh, we must understand this being must contain the attributes that make Yahweh a deserving God. It would require that this created deity be one who is of himself. The single most distinctive feature of God from all things, Yahweh, the I AM, is that He is who He is— He is of Himself. God is a se. He is dependent on no other being, and He is the only one who brings things into existence out of nothing. God, who has aseity, has never came into existence, but rather, always was. Coming into existence would mean that there was a point in time that God was not of Himself, and furthermore, it would require that God also had to have been created or brought into existence by something— which would mean He is not of Himself as He claims for Himself in Scripture by the virtue of His personal name to Moses— Yahweh.

A deity, a god or a goddess, must also be of such a status of being a se if he can be called a deity in the image of God. Now, of course, a contradiction becomes apparent— if man is a god in the image of the one God, then he must be a se, and a se things could not have come into being. However, we know that God has created human beings (I am assuming the reader assents to such an idea). This means that man as a god is also an impossibility. The reason this is so important is the concern of worship. God is to be worshipped because we derive our all from Him. As soon as we elevate ourselves to the point of being gods, we no longer owe worship to God, but owe worship to ourselves for being the progenitors of our own being. There are many other attributes of God we could talk about that the images of God lack— not only that, even the attributes that we have from God are merely finite unlike their infinitude in God. Therefore, we know that the term “image of God” does not mean that mankind receives every aspect of God’s attributes— we only receive certain communicable ones at a finite level.



The Proper View

This springboards us into the proper conceptualization of being the image of God. If our God is the only a se God, then we must understand that being an image of God is also a process of being dependent on such an a se God. Our understanding of being the images of God cannot ever deviate from our understanding of God Himself and His aseity as we are never a se and hence our every aspect of our being is completely dependent on Him. We are images of God only to the extent that He allows us to be images of God. Our identity as images of God are within us only to the extent to which God does not remove Himself from us— the moment God removes all of His grace for us to be, in any shape or form, the image of God, we cease to be so. Like all things of God’s creation, it is God’s mercy and grace that allows us to enjoy our given status.

Often, we use the identity of being the images of God to justify the dignity of human beings. This is most agreeable. As man is created in the image of God, to respect and to honor the representative creation that is to reflect the Most High is an endeavor we all ought to pursue. This is great news for mankind. This means that as long as man retains his status as the image bearer of God, he remains of value and of deserving dignity and respect. Man cannot be robbed of his dignity just because he does not live up to a certain human standard— the identity of being an image bearer of God is intrinsic to the created being of man and will remain so as long as God allows it to be so. However, we must be careful to stay within the bounds of what this entails. It is pertinent that we not elevate man to the status of deserving a respect and dignity by some value that is imparted on them of and by themselves. That is, we must understand that man, as does all things besides God that is not a se, derives his value and allotment of respect and honor, only so far as God allots it to him. No man is beyond the God that they bear the image of, and the image bearer’s value is intrinsically tied to the value of the God that he bears the image of.



A Metaphor

The metaphor I personally employ is that of a lamp— not a specific type of a lamp, but a simple vessel that has the ability to hold light. The man is a lamp and God Himself is the very light that shines forth in the darkness. This lamp is a vessel that can emanate light from itself and is built for the purposes of doing so. In one sense, the lamp is an image of the light. The lamp emanates the light, represents the light, and often acts out certain communicable functions of the light. It has a certain value and an honor of being that vessel that can reflect its creator. No other object (for the sake of this argument) shares in the special honor of shining some of the communicable attributes of the said light, but the lamp. Other objects, in their existence, may be affected by and hence may showcase the light’s effect in some fashion such as through the exhibition of a color, but other objects may not shine the light in its brilliance. A flower radiates its beauty by the work of the light reflecting off its petals to illustrate the beauty of the light itself but cannot make other things to shine. A red apple may entice one to think it delicious by its shining in the light, but it may not shine light itself. In such a way, there is a certain level of value and dignity to the object of the lamp. However, although the lamp enjoys these certain privileges in its existence and function, it is not the light itself. It can neither create the light by its own subsistence nor employ something other than the light to act as the light. A lamp cannot do its job if the light chooses not to emanate from the lamp. Though the lamp is valuable because of its being and existence, it is only valuable because there is something that gives it value— the light. The lamp has a certain level of value and dignity because of an external substance that grants those qualities.



Loss of Value

Now, can this lamp completely lose its value? It certainly can. A lamp loses its value utterly if and when two conditions are met. The lamp must first lack both its emanation and its capacity. That is, we are separating the lamp’s emanation of light and the lamp’s capacity to hold the light. First, the lamp must no longer emanate light— this means that the lamp is not doing its job. The lamp does not shine from itself the light that it was built to shine. However, this, I believe, is not a good enough reason to render the lamp of completely no value. The lamp still holds a certain capacity and potential to hold and shine light from itself and a value in its potential to be of use in the future. Therefore, the second criteria must be met. The lamp must lose its capacity to emanate light. The lamp must not be able to hold light even if the light wished to be emanated from the lamp. Not only that, but the light must be at such incapacity and inability to ever emanate light in the future— it must be utterly broken and irredeemable.

So, how is mankind categorized in such an understanding of imago Dei as a lamp? Well, for one, we are most certainly broken from the effects of the Fall, and we are also out of communion with God (by we, I mean mankind as sinners). In the metaphor of the lamp. Upon the sin of Adam, the lamp has lost the light, and the lamp has also lost the ability to shine the light. Upon a quick glance, this seems to check the boxes for mankind losing all value if we were to adhere to the metaphor of the lamp. However, upon analysis, we must realize that the second criteria, the requirement concerning the capacity of the lamp, is not met. As I had said earlier, not only the capacity of the lamp for the light must be destroyed, but also the lamp must become irredeemable from its state of incapacity. Mankind has certainly fallen to a point of total depravity that reckons him depleted in his every part to hold the light, but there is still hope for the man because he has not become utterly depraved— depraved to the point of no redemption. Therefore, the man still holds a level of dignity and respect as a result of his still existent potential to be redeemed and to emanate the light as it ought to. The lamp is still a lamp despite being broken in all the places with no hope aside from its maker to fix it and for the light to decide to shine from it. Man holds value in his intrinsic identity as a broken image bearer who may be redeemed in the future.

Just to solidify the metaphor, I want to clarify one additional thing about the state of the fallen lamp. Though we no longer shine the light as we ought to, we must remember that even a broken lamp can sometimes reflect light. Though it cannot emanate light in the likeness of the light as it ought to, it can certainly reflect light in a glinting hope when the light chooses to impart itself for moments. Yet, certainly, though the lamp may still be remembered and treated with a certain level of respect and even as a representation of the light, it lacks power, authority, and even likeness to the original state.



Redemption

As we know, God walked in the garden with man (Genesis 3:8). This means that God was in communion with and was in fellowship with man. From this, in our metaphor, we can see that the lamp had light to emanate from itself. However, man sins and is cast out of the garden. He is broken and Godless.

Of course, the story does not end there. It was God’s grace that man did not become utterly depraved and absolutely hopeless. God allows man to become totally depraved, but not irredeemable. God gives hope in the protoevangelium of Genesis 3:15, and accomplishes that promise through Jesus in the perfect life in incarnation, the sinless death of the crucifixion, and the triumphant victory of the resurrection. Through the grace presented to the Christian, the Christian is redeemed from his status as a broken lamp to one that once again shines the light that is God. The Christian becomes a true image bearer of God as he was originally designed to be. However, I believe that the redeemed image bearer is a far greater representative of God than the original image bearer. Adam, in the garden, was presented with the covenant of works. He needed to keep the law given by God to have dominion over the earth and to multiply, and to not eat from the tree of the knowledge of good and evil. This meant that Adam, the original image bearer, was to do work to achieve salvation before the fall and that was up to his own doing— his own effort and his own will. This, of course, proved to be a task we failed. However, for the Christian, the redeemed image bearer, God lives within us as He has bought our salvation to heal and to restore us from death to life. Furthermore, we now can do all things good by the power of the Holy Spirit working in us doing a co-work with us in our continued sanctification. This is not to say that Adam did not have God with Him to do good originally, but that to obey God and hence reflect God in his obedience, Adam had to do it through a perfect obedience by his own power.

To clarify— coming back to our lamp metaphor, the original image bearer can be likened to a lamp that had within it, a mechanism that took in the light of God to shine it back out to the world. That is, the light was not within Adam, but the light walked among him and Adam needed to only always be pointed towards the light as his source. The lamp needed to make sure that it would not lose sight of the light— it needed to make sure that it would not try to be its own light. However, we know that the lamp lost sight of the true light— failed to trust that the light was good— and sought its own light. The redeemed image bearer, in comparison, has a light that is more direct and unavoidable in nature. For the redeemed image bearer, the light does not only walk with the image bearer, but the light lives within him. The light dwells in the lamp so that that lamp cannot ever lose sight of the light that is within him. There is such a close, intimate dwelling of the light within the lamp that it is a more perfect representation of the light than ever before. Of course, before glorification in heaven, the lamp is still in the paradox of the broken-yet-redeemed position, and though the lamp must, in its sanctification, fight continually to let the light within is shine rather than attempt to create its own light, the victory is guaranteed— the lamp will be more glorious and be a better representative than even before the Fall. Upon the resurrection and glorification the lamp will be redeemed to a more perfect capacity and emanation. From this, we see that the lamp is not merely restored but redeemed to a greater honor, dignity, and respect.



Human Dignity, Justice, and Christian Charity

This is why, as image bearers, we must be careful to determine how we are to serve and to work with other image bearers. Every image bearer, including the ones still unredeemed, have a certain value and dignity to them because their identity as images of God has not perished yet. Though they are broken and ineffective, by the mercies of God, they are still lamps that hold the potential and hope of being restored to shine the light they were designed to represent— in such a way, they still hold the dignity of the image bearer. However, as we have discussed, because the lamp is given value and dignity by the light, when we talk about human dignity that is existent in all human beings, we must be able to address it in relation to the giver of dignity. Though the identity of the image bearer is intrinsic, the value that comes as a result of that identity is existent only to the extent that God has decided to retain hope in it.

I believe that as Christians, we must treat fellow image bearers with dignity and this is primarily shown in our duty to treat people charitably and also justly. Often the idea of charity is intermingled with the idea of justice (especially by the liberal Christian circles), but the two are distinct things. By Christian charity, I mean the impression of compassion and mercy upon a people who do not necessarily deserve it. This is separate from justice.

Justice is the execution of a just ruling upon an individual based on the righteousness or wickedness of the said individual. Justice is not the action of treating a fellow neighbor well or the act of loving or caring for another based on his need. When the Bible talks about justice, it is always in the context of the impartial ruling of the legal standing of a human being. The poor and the rich both, as equivalent images of God, will be judged according to the same standard and must not be robbed of their just reward and must not be excused from their just punishment. Justice of man is to show that all men are treated with a certain expectation to do good (abide by the law). This reflects the justice of God which judges based on whether or not the man followed the decree of God for the man to be a proper image of God. That is, as the lamp was created to hold light, a lamp will be judged according to whether or not the lamp did its job or not. Charity, on the other hand is an action of providing merciful and compassionate care and disposition towards those who are in need. The recipients of such charity are characterized not by deservedness, but by neediness. Why is this distinction between justice and charity so important? Ultimately they are both good things are they not? Well, yes, of course, however, we must understand that all good things are good because they are a reflection of who God is— the utmost good. If we understand that justice and charity are driven from our understanding of God, then we must understand that by confusing the two we may yield a comprehension of God that is flawed and harmful.

You see, if we misunderstand justice, we may start demanding justice for things that we do not deserve. We must hold to the fact that justice is what we under the law not based on our needs, wants, or our conditions, but based on what we have done in our actions. If we are practicing proper justice, when there is a judgement against the poor versus the rich, we will not take their wealth into account in that judgement but decide only based on their actions. This proper justice is, as are all things that are good, supposed to model the justice that God desires. God’s justice is perfectly just when He presides over His heavenly courtroom because He is perfectly impartial as to the conditions or the qualities of the defendant, but is perfectly perceptive of the righteous and wicked deeds of the defendant. To demand justice before God is to demand to be judged according to our righteousness and wickedness— not according to what we what we would or would not want. It is not favorable to ask God to be just in His provision of what we want in our daily lives because if God was acting purely out of justice in His providence towards us, we would all receive punishment, not what we desire and need for survival and flourishing. Our justice towards fellow image bearers ought to be the same as well. When we act with justice towards our fellow men, we are acting with impartiality and dealing with them according to what they deserve in their righteous or wicked deeds. Our justice, as image bearers, should shine the justice of God.

Now, I want to talk about charity. By charity, I mean the act of caring for and providing for a neighbor, a fellow image bearer, for the sake of their neediness. The Christian has a duty to provide to the fellow man a certain respect and honor as a result of that man having dignity resulting from him being an image of God. Charity, I hold with a great emphasis in this essay because I believe that when we are talking about human dignity and our charitable actions toward mankind, there is something extremely pertinent at hand regarding the greater redemptive history. Charity is so important to the Christian because by properly portraying charity, we are reminded of the greatest charity the world has ever witnessed. It is charity, not justice, the merciful grace of the loving God, that we experience when God redeems His fallen image bearers from death to life. Though the sinner had committed deathly sin, he was spared of being irredeemably broken (for he was only totally depraved, not utterly/absolutely depraved), then, he was given the opportunity and hope of revival. It was the neediness of the sinner that required the merciful God to allow the Son to take on the sin of mankind to bring the dead to life. It was neediness, not deservedness. Christian charity is so important because it allows us to reminisce and pay forward the greatest charity of the cross, yet the world often turns charity into a form of justice. The world turns charity into a “social justice” as if charity is something that we deserve rather than are mercifully granted. When the Christian practices charity, he must practice it with a desire to commit acts of mercy upon those who are needy with compassion that is derivative of the compassion and passion of Christ on Calvary.

To misunderstand charity as justice would be to misunderstand the common grace of continued physical life as entitlement, and the cross as an act of justice instead of the mercy it was. To commit such an act would be to render ourselves worthy or deserving of the sacrifice of the Son of God— which would be to say that the lamp never lost the light or the capacity to hold the light. Charity reflects the lovely cross and the cross was not justice but mercy for us. We deserve justice which says we deserved death on that cross, yet we received charity— mercy— by the death of Jesus on that cross. As recipients of the propitiation on the cross, the Christian has a duty to be charitable, but the recipient is not deserved of the said charity. I want this to be extremely clear. The Christian has the utmost duty and heavy responsibility of charity towards the neighbors because of how much he has freely received and because he realizes that the person he is relating to is one of God’s image, but the neighbor cannot and may not demand out of his status and condition to say that he is deserving of such charity by his own righteousness.

The duty of the Christian and the undeserved nature of the recipient are two different ideas, but they both share the same core motivation from the message of the cross. The giver must be charitable because he has been abundantly given by the Savior of the promise of eternal life and fellowship with God and the saints. Furthermore, the giver is charitable because he is being charitable towards a fellow image of God who still has potential for redemption (or is already redeemed) to emanate the light of God— the charitable Christian views the fellow image bearer as valuable for the being that he has that could be redeemed by God. The recipient, on the other hand, understands the charity as help for those in a position of neediness (as all sinners are) and receiving without preconditional deservedness. The need to treat other human beings with dignity in charity comes not at the deservedness of the individual, but the duty of the charitable Christian to have compassion as Christ had compassion.



Two Types of Charity

True Christian charity exists in two different realms. The different realms are bifurcated into those who believe and those who do not. Both are based on the same notion that as image bearers, both the believer and the unbeliever ought to be treated with mercy as shown to us by Christ. However, there are differences in what charity conveys to each of the two types of charity (or rather recipients of charity).

Towards the believer, Christian charity is an act of enjoyment of the brothers and sisters in Christ. We care for the fellow believers because with them we are one family and one body. When one part of the body is either hurt or is in need, it is the Christian’s charitable duty to love them as Christ loves His Church. It is fundamentally the acting out the heart of Christ for the Church that He was willing to lay down His life for. It is still not something the Church deserves, but it is certainly something that the Church has an absolute duty towards in order to remember and to share in the benefits of God’s merciful charity to us.

The other branch of Christian charity is towards the unbelievers. This is the branch of charity that is aimed at the outside world that is still antagonistic against God. The charity we show to the world is different from the brotherly love we show to the believers in that to the world, we commit a charity that is fundamentally evangelistic in nature. To clarify, I do not mean that we need to repeat the verbal Gospel message with each act of charity that we do, but that each act of charity we do ought to contain the Gospel in its very composition. Even the simple act of providing for the poor should be an act that desires fundamentally to share the act of Christ in providing Himself as wealth for the spiritually bankrupt. The act of feeding the hungry should be an act that seeks to show the Lamb who gave Himself as the bread of life for the spiritually starving. The Beatitudes and the many signs and miracles of Jesus testify of the charity of the cross, and if even our Savior’s charitable works in His incarnate ministry (not to mention all of the Old Testament mercies) are acts of charity that feed the undeserving, needy people, then our acts of charity ought to mirror His for no disciple is greater than his master.



The Result of Charity

Now, bringing together all that we have discussed regarding charity, if we are to believe what we have read, in either of the types of charity, we must take care that the goal and result of our charity is not a utopia or a betterment of human lives. Yes, you read that correctly— the Christian effect on the world in charity is not one of bettering the world! Rather, the purpose of charity is to show a divine community one in Body or to show the Gospel to a world that still does not know it is spiritually hungry, poor, and dead. If at any point Christian charity loses its purpose, it ceases to be Christian charity and becomes a vain, man-centered attempt at creating a kingdom on shifting sand. “But we are to bring the Kingdom of Heaven here down to earth!”, one may complain. To this, I would strongly affirm, “Yes, absolutely,”, but would add, “but, do you know what the Kingdom of Heaven is?”. We must define the Kingdom of Heaven not by our own desires of what a utopia would look like, but by what is Biblically understood as the Kingdom of Heaven. The Kingdom of Heaven is not a well oiled system that churns out an endless supply of ambrosia and nectar or a human nation with perfect governmental programs and education. The Kingdom of Heaven, or the Kingdom of God, is where God is the King— it is in the name. If this is true, what it means to bring the Kingdom of Heaven down to earth is to bring the Gospel to those who have not heard it in hopes that they may have God in themselves so that God would establish His Kingdom wherever His subjects are. Now, to bring the Kingdom then means to share the Gospel and to live in community with the believers in a way that shares the truths about God.

We must take care to distance ourselves from the idea that somehow charity is about achieving some benefit for the people of the world. That may certainly happen as a byproduct of the faithful charity work that Christians do, but it is certainly not our intent. It is quite concerning that the world, including so-called Christians, clamor on about the need to provide an equity of results for all populations of the world. The natural problem with this, of course, is that with such a definition of charity, the intent and purpose of charity is twisted to not be about the expansion of the Kingdom of God through evangelistic compassion, but it becomes a desire to create a utopia apart from God by our own means. If at any point, the individual focuses on the results of charity as the equitable distribution of comforts to a dying world (or worse, an attempt to fix the dying world), the individual rejects that God is the only solution to the world.

You see, God had presented the solution to the brokenness of the image bearer. The true solution, as we discussed prior, is that in Christ’s power, the lamp is redeemed to be of greater brilliance than ever before. Not only that, but through Jesus’s own words and through an entire book of the Bible dedicated to the future plans of God (Revelation), we are told that a New Heaven and New Earth would be established to replace this crumbling old earth. Our utopia is one where God is present and God is bearing His light for all lamps to shine with. As image bearers, we are supposed to embrace our new redemption and our future hope because these plans will allow us to accomplish our task of being good bearers of God’s image. However, the worldly image bearer dismisses this plan of God to establish for himself an image of the world according to his own image. Of course, the problem is that the world a lightless lamp can imagine can only be a dark, lightless world. It may perhaps have a slight imprint of a memory about what light looked like, but it only has itself to rely on in creating its own light— which is an impossibility. This is why the idea of “helping people for people’s sake (without bringing God into it)” is ridiculous. The purpose and goal of an image bearer is to bear the image of God, and if one were to help the image bearer for the image bearer’s sake without the factor of God, one would not be helping the image bearer. Instead, if anyone were to help an image bearer, he ought to help the image bearer for the sake of the image bearer knowing the God he is supposed to emanate. Only when the image bearer knows God, can he truly be helped to be what he is supposed to be.

If we are ever to commit to true charity as all Christians are called to, we must most assuredly dismiss any attempts to redefine charity to be human-centric. Such humanistic approach to charity only furthers the sinful, worldly self-dependence that was first committed by our forefather Adam in the garden. We must reject wholeheartedly the notion that man ought to provide an equitable society to the world— such an ideology is one that misinterprets the Biblical goal of charity to redeem man to His creator. Rather, it replaces the Gospel with a human idolatry that focuses on the man being the light itself, not the lamp. We must treat the image bearer with dignity without removing the highest dignity due to the source of all dignity— God Himself.



Conclusion

Man is called the image of God because he is to be a representative that shares the light of God to the world that needs it. However, the lamp can never be the light itself, and in order for the lamp to do the work that it is crafted to accomplish, it cannot forsake the source of its brilliance— much less its creator. The image of God must realize that he is of no value if not for the God he is to emanate.

Therefore, the Christian concept of charity, in which the understanding of the image of God is most pertinent, must be defined within the proper understanding of what it means to provide mercy and compassion towards fellow image bearers. The image bearer has the greatest definition of charity when looking upon the sacrifice on Calvary, and he must take care to reflect the definition of charity that has been given us, not one that the image bearer seeks to define with his own light. Christian charity must be defined by its adherence to the Biblical definition. Firstly, it must be done because we respect that the man is an image bearer of the Most High and therefore has intrinsic qualities of being an image of God that burdens the Christian with a duty to be respectful to the bearer. Secondly, it must be done as an act of mercy and compassion towards those not deserving, but rather needy— the lamp was completely broken and lacking light, not doing its job, undeserving, and hopeless if not for the light-giver. Thirdly, it must be done with the correct intent of bringing the Kingdom of Heaven to earth by the means of showing a saintly community (for the believers), and by exemplifying the greatest act of charity ever shown in the redemptive history by the one Lord and Savior Jesus Christ— our light. Christian charity must never be defined by humanistic misconceptions. Firstly, charity must not be done with an insistence that somehow the man is valuable apart from God Himself— no lamp is valuable if it cannot ever hold the light (at this point it is no lamp at all). Secondly, charity must not be done by redefining it to be a form of justice— such an act assumes that the recipient of charity is somehow deserving rather than needy of the acts of charity. Thirdly, charity must not be done with the purposes of bettering the world— no human power will be able to restore the world, but only God has promised His redemption of the world; we hold to that promise.
