Guilt and Consequence

A Response to the Concept of Corporate Guilt/Responsibility to Sin

We must separate and distinguish guilt and consequence properly in order for us to perceive a Biblical understanding of sin. Sin is the trespassing and debt owed to the Creator, and all mankind is guilty of it. This sin results in certain consequences. Guilt testifies of the standing an individual has before God while consequence is the evidence of that guilt. Guilt is more than just a feeling, but it is the legal and forensic status before the judgement of God. The one who has guilt is the one who has actively chosen against God’s goodness for the option of his own judgement of good and evil— he has chosen out of his free agency against the Holy One. One may easily not have the feelings of guilt but still remain in the objective state of being guilty— in fact, this is the state of the majority of the world. Justice requires the treatment of the individual based on their guilt, the standing, and this treatment is what we may understand as the consequence. The individual reaps what he has sowed.

Furthermore, what we must understand about guilt is that guilt is a standing before God. It is not some metaphysical object as the feeling of guilt would be. No, rather guilt is tied to the individual regarding his personal status before justice— ultimately, the divine justice of God. When approaching the throne of God on the last day, we will stand before God alone. The Christians will have our representative, Christ, in place of us, but ultimately, we will be judged individually based on our own actions in the course of our individual lives. We will not be able to stand with our parents, or spouse, or children, or friends on that last day— not that such an alignment would allot us any benefits. Our guilt before God will be exposed for the judge to see, and the consequence of our guilty standing will either be the second death, or an eternal life. Justice in the eyes of God is the accurate and precise reward or punishment given according to the guilt or righteousness that remains in a man. Judgement is the proper and faithful attendance and application of such justice.

Human judgement models the divine judgement in its understanding of guilt and consequence as well. In a Bible-informed courtroom, every defendant is held responsible for his own guilt and receives a due penalty as an individual. How horrid would it be for the child of a criminal to receive a death-penalty for the crimes of the father? To claim that those who have not committed the crime are responsible and hence guilty is the definition of the subversion of justice. The individual, as distinctly defined in God’s creation purpose, is personally responsible for the actions he commits even in the human court because the human court does not exist outside of God’s purpose, but is established to represent the divine court. Only the criminal is guilty before the judgement and only the criminal will receive the direct punishment as a consequence of his crimes— no one else.

If we understand such a truth, we must deny and extinguish any notions of guilt and responsibility belonging to anyone but the individual. There is no such thing as a “corporate responsibility1” or “corporate guilt” attributed to a group of people. To be clear, I am not claiming that a group of people cannot commit a crime or sin together— sin corporately, but rather, I am saying that there is no Biblical concept of an affiliation or group of people being held responsible or guilty for the sins of the one person of that group. Such a claim would be to imply that God would hold the sins of the father over the son, or even worse, it may mean that God would hold the sins of a criminal over someone who just happens to be his friend. To make a claim that an individual is responsible for the sins of other people is a preposterous and miscreant proposition. If truly the human comprehension of justice is to mirror the divine attribute of justice (that is, not to the fullest degree, but we must never attribute a false positive to our understanding of justice), we must understand that what we believe about justice is the justice that we believe exists in God. If so, we must not go against the very Word of God that says that God will not hold the son responsible for the father and the father responsible for the son (Deut 24:16; Eze 18:20). To think that justice would hold others responsible for an affiliate’s crimes would be also to think that God’s justice would hold others responsible for an affiliate's sins— it is utterly preposterous.

In addition to the denigration of God’s attribute of justice, the belief of a corporate guilt or sin is treacherous to the Biblical teachings of human will and responsibility. Corporate sin betrays a suggestion that the individual committing the sin is not the one fully liable for the specific sin in question. It is the individual free agency and choice that engendered the resulting sin. It is at no point the fault or the responsibility of anyone else that the individual committed the said sin. We must not commit the same sin as Adam in saying, “The woman whom you gave to be with me, she gave me fruit of the tree, and I ate.” (Gen 3:12). Eve had her own sin that had reverberating consequences, but the guilt was individually applied according to their own sins. To consider that other people are responsible for another’s sin suggests that the particular guilt of the sin in question must be shared amongst a corporation. Naturally, the sharing of the guilt naturally requires the division of the intensity of the guilt among the parties involved— meaning that the individual who committed the sin is no longer fully responsible for his own actions, but the guilt may be shifted to others. We must flee from such a derelict understanding of human responsibility and the gift of free agency that excuses the self while blaming the others. This is not to say that other people do not affect us. One of the most destructive elements of sin is that it corrupts. It corrupts the man, nature, and other men. However, this is a matter of consequence, not guilt— it is a consequence that man is affected by the sins of others. When a man stands in the divine court for a sin, he stands for that sin, and along with him, others in relation to him who sinned will stand trial as well. However, these others related to the man will not stand trial for the sins of the man, but will stand trial for their own sins that may have produced the consequences that impacted the man in question. For example, Adam was pronounced guilty for listening to the voice of his wife rather than God— that was his sin, no others. Sure, he attempted to transfer the guilt to the woman, but it was not availed. On the other hand, the serpent, the devil, was convicted of his deception of the woman. The woman was convicted of trusting the serpent over God. Each affected the other in the consequences of sin, but all were convicted individually of the sins and were pronounced guilty respective to their sins.

Now, to such refutations of the concept of corporate responsibility and guilt to sin, one may interject with a consideration of the Biblical narratives where a group suffers for the sins of one or the few. What are we to make of such cases? It seems that either the group is unjustly being punished for the crimes of another or they are being held responsible for the sins of the other. There are several instances of these cases in the Bible, and those in opposition may suggest that these instances suggest and imply that such a thing as corporate guilt indeed does exist as an unjust action by God would be impossible. They cannot see a reason why other people would suffer because of the actions of the few. Such events are present in passages such as Numbers 16, Deuteronomy 23:3-8, Joshua 7:22-26, 1 Samuel 15:2-3, 2 Samuel 21:1-9, and Daniel 9:1-16.

Firstly, I would ask that the reader recognize, in all of the occurrences of such events, the scope of the justice exercised. To explain, I am asking that the individual reading these passages and interpreting them notice the judge in each of these circumstances is. One will notice in even a passing glance, that the presider over all of these cases, and the pronouncer of the consequences is indeed God Himself. The scope at which justice over the guilt of the individuals was practiced was at the divine level. Against the God of the universe had these individuals sinned, as are all sins, and God was using these special instances to illustrate the severity of the punishments for such crimes. So, what does this mean? Well, foremostly, at the surface level, it becomes quite clear to us that these are instances of divine justice and retribution for the sinful that are meant to be descriptive cases that illustrate the solemnity of divine justice— it is not meant to be a prescriptive understanding of the human justice that we, beings limited in knowledge and righteousness, practice. We need to be sure that we are not the ultimate arbiters of justice. All that the human justice system seeks to do is to inherit and model the justice that God desires in the human, flawed government. If truly the penance in a human prison could pay for the sins of a man (which is committed towards God foremost — Psalm 51:4), why would we need the divine judgement day or the punishments of eternal hellfire or the rewards of the New Heaven and the New Earth? When we look at these instances of divine justice, we must never apply them as if they can be exercised outside of God’s courtroom. Only God judges from His throne.

Now, our inability to exercise such perfect justice certainly does not excuse us from our need to understand it. We must understand divine justice in order for us to properly model it with limitations on earth. So, am I suggesting that in the divine court, those who did not commit a crime can be held responsible for the sins of another? No! That would utterly contradict my statements made prior. No, rather, as I said earlier, when contemplating these examples given us, we must enlarge our scope to the level of the divine plan of redemption— to the level of the redemptive history.

To understand, let us go back to the primal doctrine of sin. When we question why people would suffer anything at all, we must understand that any suffering is a consequence of the guilt that we hold. Mankind experiences all forms of evil because of his sin. Because he has sinned against the Holy One, and since man is separate from the very source of life, man cannot help but experience suffering. Any natural man is deserving of all suffering because he is born with a sin nature that desires evil and is guilty of the initial sin of Adam. This is the one sin all mankind is guilty of through the action of another. This original sin is imputed upon mankind because of the special federal headship of Adam over all of mankind-all of mankind was with Adam as he sinned. There has existed no other federal head besides Adam except Jesus, and hence, no other man has acted in our place. We are born with the original sin that makes us sinners who are deserving of death, not life. If this was not enough, we sin everyday— no man except our Savior Jesus has never committed a sin in His waking or slumbering. This is a critical piece of reality that we must most assuredly commit to remembrance as forgetting such guiltiness would result in our entitlement to think that we do not deserve any consequences for our guilt. No, rather we live in the perpetual state of “sinners in the hands of an angry God” as the great puritan Jonathan Edwards put it. Mankind is in the hands of a God who is so just and holy that a single ounce of sin committed against Him is deserving of being dropped into the eternal hellfire, but it is also this same God who keeps mankind in the palm of His hand, refraining from dropping them, waiting for the opportune time to judge according to His sovereign plan. If we understand man’s condition as such, we would not be concerned of the deserved nature of all to suffer the wrath of God. It is by the mercy of God that an individual stays alive on the face of this fallen planet, and no man may raise a righteous complaint against God for taking away that hand of mercy. It is of no responsibility of God that He retains His mercy upon an individual— His radiant goodness need not be fixed on a sinner deserving His righteous, just wrath. God has no reason and duty to refrain from removing His mercy even at random. The administration of justice for man from God is an act of merely removing the bulwark of mercy that was holding back His just wrath.

No man can complain, when referencing the passages in discussion, that somehow if a suffering man is not guilty of the particular sin in question, he is unjustly afflicted by God. When certain families of Korah’s rebellion suffered (Num 16:32), when the Ammonites and Moabites were excluded (Deut 23:3), when Achan’s family was stoned (Joshua 7:25), when all of things Amalekite were devoted to destruction (1 Sam 15:3), when the sons of Saul were hanged (2 Sam 21:9), when the later generations of Israel remained in exile (Dan 9), God was not unjustly punishing innocent people, God was merely expediting the wrath of God or giving a taste of what the genuine wrath of God could be likened to.

This, however, still leaves the question of reason. That is, why would God do such a thing as removing mercy from those related to the sinner? We may understand that God has the right to do so, but how does doing so exemplify God and improve man’s understanding of Him? Quite simply, I believe it is to show the holiness and goodness of God in contrast to the utter destructive and corrupting consequences of sin. In some occasions, God releases His hand of mercy even on the whole of a community to show what the interconnected world of sin is due for when the ugliness of sin is revealed by the derelict actions of a sinner.

To better understand, let us start from a human example of the consequences of sin. For example, let us conjure up a situation where a man commits a crime punishable by a long sentence in prison. What are the consequences? Rather obviously, the man suffers the consequence of losing his freedom— this mirrors the loss of free will (ability to choose good or evil inclusive of the actual effective ability) that Adam experienced in the Garden after his sin. Furthermore, the man suffers a detachment from the good things of the world that were once obtainable. In addition to all of this, the man may suffer from feelings of guilt. Yet, the consequences do not end there. When Adam sinned in the Garden of Eden, the relationship between man and the whole world was broken, not just with God. Adam was to be in enmity with nature and with man as well. Yet, man still remained connected to the world— this thereby propagating the symptoms of sin across to nature and man. When a man sinned, he would impact both nature and the fellow man. Returning to our imprisoned man, this man also impacts those outside of himself. The consequences of his crimes permeate to his family, friends, and even fellow inmates and prison guards. Let us focus on just the family at this moment. The spouse of the man must now provide for the family and must do a work originally assigned to the male head of the family. The children of the man must grow up without the spiritual head of the family and will lack guidance in areas where paternal exhortation is naturally needed— this often has the consequence of tending the children towards the same crimes committed by the father. Not only that, but the child may view the justice system with contempt and may even confuse the crimes of the father as being right. The consequences of a man in a fallen world naturally propagates to all— man is not isolated when the consequence of sin befalls him. Are we to blame the judicial system? No, for the judicial system is merely acting out the justice required for the man of crime. Only the man who committed the crime and the fallen world in their respective sins are to be held responsible. If the world was not fallen and in their own sins, they would not feel the consequences, however, as it is fallen, even the people aside from the man feel the reverberating consequences of the man’s sins.

Bringing us back to the divine judgements that we see in the Biblical passages in discussion, the same logic must be applied at a higher level. If the (hypothetical) man’s actions has consequences for his crimes that reverberate throughout the fallen creation, why would we shy away from the idea that the sin of one man would have consequences that reverberate to his family if not his whole community? In fact, we must not forget that it is still under God’s sovereignty that our hypothetical example of the man in the human judicial system would have such effects on his community. God allowed such suffering of the man’s family— in fact, since we are devoted to the sovereignty and omniscience of God, God willed it. The consequences, not the guilt, of another’s sin can be felt by another even if that individual has not committed that specific sin because ultimately every individual is a sinner in a connected, broken world. Bringing our concepts of the deservedness of punishment for all men and the understanding of the intensity of and interconnectedness of the sinful man to the fallen world together, we must understand that the sinner will sometimes experience the expediting of suffering as a result of someone else’s sin, but when they do suffer, they are suffering in punishment of their own personal guilt as God removes His hand of mercy away.

If we accept it as so, we must view the events of the Biblical passages in the same illumination. When Israel was still exiled for the sins of their fathers, they were feeling the repercussions of sin. When Daniel prays in chapter 9 of his namesake book, he is praying for forgiveness of the guilts of our fathers— for God to endow even greater mercy upon the exiled Israel that is still feeling the reverberating consequences of the ancestral Israelites. Daniel says, “we”, not because the guilt of his fathers belongs to him, but because Daniel recognizes that he is in no place of entitlement to receive blessings, as he is also a sinner the same as his fathers. Daniel says, “we”, because he is begging for mercy from the consequences of the sins of the forefathers. In the same way, when the sons of Saul are hanged because God is still angry about the sins of Saul, it is not that the sons are held responsible for Saul’s sins (though they have their own sins to be held accountable for), but it is that the sons are inflicted with the thorns of Saul’s grievous deeds. God is showing His justice by showing that just as Saul killed off the families of the Gibeonites, so would his family be killed off. In justice, an eye for an eye still applies. Because Israel had made a promise with the Gibeonites (Joshua 9), God expected Israel to keep it just as God had kept His promises with equally deceitful and unobedient nation of Israel. The punishment was towards Saul, not towards the sons— the sons were merely impacted by the consequences of their father’s sins. The same goes for the Amalekites in 1 Samuel 15:3. There was a quickening, a hastening, of the deserved wrath of God upon the Amalekites because of the consequences of the few Amalekite men who had opposed Israel. The same is the case for the Ammonites and Moabites in Deuteronomy 23:3.

In the case of Korah’s rebellion, the holiness of priesthood decreed to be for the line of Aaron (Exodus 28:1) was threatened, and in the case of Achan, the holiness of the people of Israel was threatened. As one can see, in both cases, the holiness of God’s chosen people were of concern. The holiness of God is particularly pertinent to God’s justice and righteousness. God can judge because He is utterly free from sin and guilt unlike mankind and the world— He is holy, set apart, utterly different from the world. God expects the same of His chosen people and decrees them to be holy. When individuals not only break His Laws but contaminate and corrupt the holiness of God, God will make sure that the guilt of the perpetrators will be punished to the fullest degree. I want to be clear here, the one perpetrating the sin is still the only one who is guilty, however, due to the immensity of his sin, the consequence also is immense, resonating down to his entire posterity. Once again, this is merely a hastening of the punishment already due to all men, but for the line that has been subjected to death, it is also the loss of any hope that the lineage may be transplanted from the federal headship of Adam to the federal headship of Jesus. As certain heads of families in Korah’s rebellion and Achan had chosen to define their own holiness which was not God’s holiness, they would be given what they sought in setting themselves apart— they would be set apart from the goodness of God forever.

Through such examples in the Bible, we are able to understand that the consequences of sin run deeper than the individual guilty of the sin, but the guilt of the individual remains within the individual who committed the crime. God reveals the intensity of the ugliness of sin by His removal of mercy to allow us to become aware of the evils that our deceitful hearts so desire. Such vastness of the consequences of sin may seem a depressing and entrapping concept, but there is hope. As I had briefly mentioned above, there is the opportunity and hope of being transplanted from the world of sin and suffering to the heavenly realm of grace. Though we were once represented and bound in the tree of our federal head Adam who determined not only the consequent maladies of our lives but our very guilty standing, for all who put their faith in Jesus Christ, there is the good news that they will be affixed to the tree of life that is Christ Himself— Jesus becomes our perfect, sinless federal head. Even in the example of Korah’s rebellion, we see that mercy was offered to the sons of Korah who had relented from joining their father (Numbers 26:11). By not joining the rebellion of the world against God, Korah’s sons had joined themselves to the family of God and hence had received their guarantee of eternal mercy even to the degree of having the honor of being used by the Spirit to write many of the psalms in the Book of Psalms. The same hope is given to all mankind. If any man would put his faith in Christ and Christ only to be a part of the holy family of God, he is removed from the damnation of the world to be placed into the folds of God’s grace and eternal mercy. This does not mean that the Christian will not encounter suffering in this fallen world— as long as we are still in the world, we will be subject to certain consequences of it. However, if we are eternally secure and the stains of our guilt are forever removed, we will rest assured knowing that our experiences of the consequences of sin not committed by ourselves are not because of the guilt that remains within, but because of our roles as imitators of Christ— including His suffering of consequences undeserved of Him. As Daniel’s prayer so longed for in chapter 9, we have been redeemed once again to God’s side as His children though once we were stray rebels thirsting for the blood of God.

Our proper understanding of guilt and consequence allows us to define the Biblical understanding of justice accurately. We must understand that God will not hold another guilty for the sins of one as He promises he would not, and yet God is merciful to us in His decision to not withdraw His mercy upon the majority of the world. Even further still, God is merciful to expand His invitation of grace that they who are regenerated to receive it may become living proofs of both God’s mercy and justice.
