Justice

The Proper Grasp of It and the Pertinence of It

The Bible speaks quite abundantly about the topic of justice and judgement. Whether it be to show that God is “exalted in judgement” (Isaiah 5:16), or that He is enthroned upon justice (Psalm 89:14) or that He loves justice (Isaiah 61:8), it can be seen that our God is a just God— He is always judging properly according to His character. Furthermore, God requires justice from us. God desires that man “Do justice” (Jeremiah 22:3), and He uses men as judges for ruling over cases of unrighteous deeds of man— such we see in the establishment of the judges (through Moses’s father-in-law) for aiding Moses in his duties (Exodus 18:26), and even in Solomon’s famous judgement regarding the two women (1 Kings 4:28). Hence, we must come to affirm that at the substratum, justice is a theological issue that manifests itself (1) in our understanding of who God is and (2) in our given duty in life on this earth to be more like God. Now, if we are to treasure and practice justice, we must then understand what justice is in the first place. We must come to grasp the Biblical definition of justice that compels us to understand God and be more like Him.


Definition of Justice

Justice is the proper dispensation of consequences in regards to the deeds of the individual under review (Psalm 62:12; Romans 2:6; 2 Corinthians 5:10). Judgement is the proper exercise of such justice. It is important to note that justice is, as colloquially phrased, “blind”. What this means is that justice is blind to the backgrounds and conditions exterior to the deeds that belong to the individual (1 Peter 1:17). When there is judgement, only what the individual has done in his deed are under review, and nothing else. It does not matter the appearance or familial traces of the individual— only the deeds that this individual has committed are under direct review. Justice requires that the individual receive only what he deserves, and that he is deprived of all that he does not deserve. The criminal who has committed a heinous crime must receive the due punishment for it, and the saint who has done a righteous deed ought to be rewarded for his actions. It was justice that Adam deserved a condemnation to death upon his crime of disobedience in eating the fruit from the tree of the knowledge of good and evil (Genesis 2:16-17; Genesis 3:14-19). It was justice that destruction came upon Israel as a result of their evil towards the LORD (Isaiah 3:13). It was justice that brought the same destruction upon Judah (Jeremiah 5:7). It is the same justice that will bear fruit on the day of judgement (Isaiah 24:6; Malachi 4:1; Revelation 20:11-15).


Justice in Relation to Righteousness

Justice is very heavily correlated with righteousness. This can be seen when we read passages of God’s lament over His people in the words of the prophets when he often uses the two words in close proximity to one another both quite literally and literarily. However, we must, with the utmost vigile observance to the text, recognize that the two are not at all equivalent. Of course, this is most recognizable from the fact that they are distinct in their vocabulary when used alongside each other (Proverbs 21:3; Isaiah 56:1; Jeremiah 22:3; Ezekiel 18:5). The reason God presents the two words in close proximity but does not equate them in their meaning is to (1) show that they are two different concepts, and (2) to show that both are closely related in the heart of God— God desires both.

The two, justice and righteousness, are distinctly different ideas— we know this from the perspicuous way in which the Scripture reveals it to us. Let us first discuss righteousness. We know righteousness to be the act of doing what is right according to the eyes of the LORD. God is the determiner of all that is righteous and what He desires is righteous because He Himself is the embodiment of righteousness. The most lucid presentation of righteousness that God has given us is through the Law given to Moses (Exodus 20-23). The laws given at Sinai give us a glimpse into the heart of God and label what deeds are indeed righteous and which are wicked in His eyes. On the other hand, justice is, as we have already discussed above, the distribution or dispensation of what is deserved of an individual based on their actions. If so, we already see both the distinction and the intimate relation of the two. We must understand that both justice and righteousness have something to do in regards to the actions or deed of the individual in question. However, we also see that righteousness is the designation endowed upon the individual who practices righteous deeds, while justice is the mechanism that rewards the individual dependent on the individual’s righteousness. In such a way, righteousness is the state of having done righteous deeds, and justice rewards such righteous deeds with good things. This is why our beloved Apostle Paul can say, “God causes all things to work together for good to those who love God” (Romans 8:28) because those who love God have been clothed with the righteousness that is foreign to them— namely, the righteousness of Christ. All those who do good are rewarded by justice what they are due. Christians know that their reward is certainly due— not out of their own works, but by Christ’s— at the end of their lives, when the work we have yet to do in this world is finished, and when Christ comes to reign in judgement and power (Revelation 20-22).

As one could see, there is an intimate relationship between righteousness and justice. The righteous deeds of the individual makes him to become a fecund tree full of great, edible fruits, while justice allows for the gathering of such fruits to the mouth of the individual as he deserves. The contrary is also true, of course, the wicked deeds (the deeds that offend God— that which God hates) of the individual make him to become a dry, withered tree upon which no fruits grow but the choking thistles and enveloping vines which will elicit the justice of God that will lay the axe at the trunk of the tree to toss its wicked members into the eternal fire (Daniel 4:14; Matthew 3:10). It is, however, absolutely pertinent that we do not commingle the two concepts in such a way that we arrive at the conclusion that the two are equivalent concepts. We must take care to not amalgamate the two concepts in such a way that they become a violently mixed concoction of oil and water.

One additional correlation between righteousness and justice that many may find to be of much confusion is the idea that acts of justice are also considered to be righteous. That is, God finds it to be a righteous deed to do justice by giving what is owed to those who deserve it. When an individual does what is required by the Law to reward the righteous, or to punish the wicked, that is justice that God considers to be good and righteous. Solomon was such an embodiment of justice in his early years (1 Kings 4:28). Furthermore, there were direct laws given by Moses regarding justice (Exodus 21:12-22:17). As we have discussed, since laws of God point to the heart of God and what He desires— outlines for us what is considered righteous— we must understand that the due retribution and stipulations in justice are what God considers to be correct in His eyes— the practice of justice is righteous. However, not all righteousness has a hand in justice. That is, we cannot say that every act of righteousness has to do with the concerns of justice and proper judgement. Other acts of righteousness, for one, may deal with mercy or holiness. We must remember that the laws of God point to the heart of God— the desires of God, and God desires not only justice, but concomitant with it, He also has a heart of providence, mercy, love, holiness, and more. Righteous deeds point to all of these, not just the justice aspect of God’s desires. For example, in Moses’s laws, we see that the slave is to be treated with certain rights and respect even after he has sold himself into slavery— this shows God’s heart of providence for His creation (Exodus 21:1-11). Furthermore, Exodus 22:18 -20 shows His desire for holiness, and verse 21-27 His mercy and love. What we see is that an act of justice, an act of paying what is owed to an individual, is only one of many righteous acts an individual could accomplish— Ezekiel 18:5 makes this abundantly clear by showing that both justice and righteousness are actions committed by a righteous man. God does particularly value justice distinctly among a people of injustice— almost as if to threaten His Day of Judgement, but that only shows us to distinguish the justice and righteousness, not merge them.

We can see with perspicuity that righteousness may not be equated with justice— nor should mercy with justice, or love with justice. These different concepts are certainly related to each other, but they are not to be equated. Justice, Biblically, is the proper dispensation of consequences in regards to the deeds of the individual under review— nothing else.


An Improper Conception of Justice

Now, one way in which one may misinterpret the Scripture to garner a malaligned definition of justice is to read certain passages that pertain to the justice given to the poor and the weak and assume a position that views justice as an act of having mercy on the poor. This unscrupulous reading of the text utterly devastates the true meanings of both mercy and justice. It renders the iron of justice to the helium of injustice as justice comes to mean that somehow those who have not necessarily done any righteous deed are now deserving of reward— this is injustice. Furthermore, it renders the crown of mercy down into a sentimental paper tiara of a child as mercy becomes something that is deserved by everyone. Justice is the giving of rewards and punishments based on the deserved nature of the individual, while mercy is the provisioning of what is due to the self to other individuals. Justice says, “we must give what is due to the man”, while mercy says, “I will forgo my reward to be given to the unworthy”. By equating the two, we arrive at an inexcusably unbiblical idea that somehow justice says, “I will give the opposite of what is due to the man”. This is an utter perversion of justice and a denigration of an attribute of God— it is most abominable and unjust for God deserves proper glory.

For example, one may read, “Vindicate the weak and fatherless; Do justice to the afflicted and destitute.” from Psalm 82:3, and in utter confusion, interpret the text as “we must do good to the poor as this is justice” — this is an irresponsible, contextless reading of the sacred Scripture! If one were to have merely read the verse before, the individual would have recognized that the proclamation of complaint is one regarding the unjust ruling of a judge in favor of the wicked at the expense of the poor and helpless who are not able to defend against injustice (Psalm 82:2-4). Furthermore, if one were to be dutiful enough to read the entire chapter of merely eight verses, they would come to see that the entirety of the psalm is a plea to God for Him to come as a judge so that He would “judge the earth” (Psalm 82:8). God here is desiring justice from injustice; He is not defining justice as merely doing good to people. However, people continue to abuse passages such as these to be contorted to mean that God is supporting a certain definition of justice that means the equality of provisions to all. It seems to be quite common that justice becomes redefined as “equality of results in quality of being” rather than the “equal treatment under the law”. Justice has been redefined to mean somehow that if a society has not achieved an egalitarian distribution of benefits to all individuals, injustice has been sown. This, of course, is quite contrary to the Biblical understanding of justice as justice requires the dispensation of consequences. Further still, even mercy becomes bedridden with ailments in such an understanding of justice. Mercy no longer becomes the gracious act of provision that mirrors the mercy of God, but it becomes an entitlement for an undeserving people.

The Bible is full of God’s desire for impartiality to the poor and the weak. And as such, there are a plethora of texts throughout the Bible regarding justice for the poor or freedom for the weak. Passages such as Isaiah 58 which call for the need for a proper fasting of the heart that desires justice and “breaking of yoke” for the poor more so than personal profit are meant to show the need for the Christians to care about the justice of those who are often ignored or even abused. The indigent, the widows, and the fatherless are among the first to be denied justice, and what God is desiring in specifically mentioning these groups is not the partiality or favor towards them in the realm of justice— they will be judged at the equivalent level as with anyone else. No, rather God is desiring justice for those who have been denied it. We must not redefine justice into “social justice” — as some form of social redistribution or realignment of social conditions. Justice merely asks the question of whether or not an individual committed a good or evil deed and rewards him based on such a standard. If a poor man was the pursuer of righteousness, he must be rewarded as such. If he, regardless of any conditions or background, was the pursuer of wickedness, he must be punished as such. To deny such distribution of consequences is injustice.

This, of course, does not rule out mercy. We must provide mercy to the poor and the weak because we— that is, anyone who has breath in their lungs— have received mercy. We all deserve nothing but the wrath of God in our sin-filled existence, but God has had mercy to refrain from dissolving our bodies at the moment of our conception. As such, we pour out mercy to the poor and the weak because we know that we were weak and poor in our very disposition when God poured out mercy upon us.


Gospel Implications of Justice

The concept of justice is most pertinent to the Gospel. It is justice that the Gospel accomplishes while lavishing upon the defendants a slew of mercy. Justice is the mechanism that stands between a sinful mankind and the loving arms of the Father. Justice, namely the justice of God, stands between man and God as the barrier that says, “you are not worthy to enter into the presence of the Holy God”. I do not mean to say that justice is bad, no, rather, I mean to show that it is good. Justice rewards the righteous and punishes the wicked, and justice shows that we deserve only the punishment as we have defied the Almighty. It is at this point that mercy comes into the picture and allows for the execution of justice while saving the wicked. Mercy does not eliminate the mechanism of justice, but diverts the consequences towards different persons. Firstly, Jesus strips us of our wickedness, and He adorns His righteousness upon the defendant who is clearly deserving of wrath so that the judgement of God would look upon the righteousness-robed defendant and proclaim the sinner enrobed as righteous. Such is the mercy of God. God has clothed us with “garments of righteousness” and wrapped us with “a robe of righteousness” (Isaiah 61:10). We see that the mercy of Christ provides for us great riches when we are being judged on our deservedness to receive a reward. Yet, the wickedness remains. Though it was stripped from our being, the wickedness remains as a debt to be paid. If this wickedness were to still envelope us, the debt would take an eternity to recompense as we have sinned against an infinite, eternal God. Oh what weight, what heavy burden sin is! Someone must take that burden upon Himself. He must cast it upon Himself and take it to the cross where He dies for the sins that He did not commit (Isaiah 53:5). Oh, who is He but Jesus! Our Savior, our Beloved, our All-in-All— my only Hope. Justice said that the sinner should pay by death; mercy took the payment and re-registered the reward to the sinner’s name.

Our proper understanding of justice is not only helpful, but necessary to the Christian faith. We must understand that justice means that we deserve what our deeds have stored up. Justice does not mean what the social justice proponents argue— it does not mean the egalitarian dispensation of blessings upon all people. No! Justice says that one will only receive the extent of what he has committed in righteousness. How could we twist and contort the definition of justice in such a way to say that we are deserving of things we did not ever earn? Not a single person on the face of this planet has earned his right to even breathe for one moment, but it was the common grace of God to provide mercy upon a people who are not His people. Not a single Christian deserves to be the heir “according to the hope of eternal life” (Titus 3:5-7), but it was the special grace of God to provide an unimaginable provision of His Breath, His Spirit, upon a people who were “yet sinners” (Romans 5:8). The Gospel demands this proper understanding of justice— we were not worthy! None! “There is none righteous” (Psalm 14:1-3; Romans 3:10-18)! Yet, mercy— oh, sweet mercy has been given to us through the person of Jesus Christ such that we were saved not according to our own deeds of righteousness, but by the mercies of God by the working of the Holy Spirit through Jesus Christ (Titus 3:5-6).

Once we dispose of the orthodox, Biblical understanding of justice, we dilute, nay, we pollute the Gospel, the Good News— the only Good News (Galatians 1:7)! Will we add to the Gospel by saying that we must add to the justice of God? Do we purport to say that there is a certain type of justice apart from the justice outlined in the Bible that we all “deserve”? We forget that there is only one definition of justice— justice according to God. Certainly all of us are deserving of— will be subject to— the justice of God— yet, none of us are deserving of the mercies of God. The mercies of God are dispensed to us as He pleases— we cannot insolently demand it from our Suzerain as mere vassals by perverting the concept of justice and labeling entitlement and self-righteousness with the two words, “social justice” — it is preposterous and outright despicable. If anyone seeks to preach a social gospel, a prosperity gospel, a liberation gospel, even if he be “an angel from heaven” (Galatians 1:8), let him be accursed— anathema!


Conclusion

Justice is so dear to us Christians because through it the beauty of the mercy of Christ is exposed— the mystery of the cross brings us to our knees due to our clear understanding of what we deserved in judgement and hence, what we have been rescued from and redeemed to. Justice is so dear to God because it magnifies Him above all things and shows Him to be a God who cannot be wronged— He will have His vengeance against the rebels. It shows His hatred of wickedness and love for righteousness. If we do not use the word “justice” properly and if we do not comprehend the concept expertly, we may be easily led astray by wolves or false shepherds. Justice has such heavy implications on the Gospel, yet never has it been more challenged of its original, orthodox meaning than ever before. We must grasp it to our heart with vigor and zealousness as the glory of God and honor due to Him is the matter at hand. Shall we let the wicked of this world wield “justice” (alone or with other words attached to it) as their self-glorifying catchphrase for diverting the Gospel and revolting against God? NO!
