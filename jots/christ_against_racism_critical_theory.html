Christ Against Racism and Critical Theory

I want to make a disclaimer here that I am writing this with a purpose to inform those who consider themselves to be Christians of the need for our hatred of racism (the thought and act of stereotyping, prejudice and mistreatment of others based on the color of their skin1) but also of critical theory which has shaped much of the modern popular debate concerning racism. This is a response to the alarming increase of Christian adoption of critical theory, a theory that is unbiblical at its basis and counter to the Gospel of the Christian religion. I am concerned about the extent to which critical theory language and basis of thought has infiltrated the modern church without raising concern for church leaders. We cannot be using the worldly means of justice to seek the worldly definition of justice— that is simply not justice. I do not seek to cover all of the many faults of critical theory, but wish to summarize what I consider critical theory’s most severe abasements of the Gospel. Please consult the plethora of resources available in describing the incompatibility of the Gospel with critical theory as present in the footnotes2.

With the issues of racism— the denigration of man, an image of God, based on the arbitrary physical difference of the color of one’s skin— the world has become inflamed with indignation towards the unjust actions committed towards those of our black image-bearers. It has become undeniable that there are problems with how many Americans often stereotype God-designed people of darker shades in a light that is not loving or reflective of God’s heart towards His creation.

It is an utter atrocity that man, a creation of the Almighty, would commit racism by considering that another man, an equally beautiful creation of the Almighty (Genesis 1:26), as of lesser value or as deserving of undignified manner of treatment based on their physical appearance. We must understand that such behavior is foremostly an insult to the Lord who has crafted His creation with a delicate hand and continues to care for that said creation with an intensity that He would even know the number of hairs on his head (Luke 12:7). The concept of multiple “races” is in itself a socially constructed view that takes miniscule genetic differences3 to divide the one human race as derived from Adam (Acts 17:26). Racism is a sin of hating our fellow image bearers for looking different from us.


The Evils of Racism

Before I go any further, I desperately want to make clear the destructive nature of racism as a sin. We must understand that racism is not merely a sin because it makes people feel rotten or even because it physically hurts people. Above all, as are all sins, racism is a sin because it is an affront to God. As we discussed above, it is firstly an insult to God regarding His creation that He saw fit to call, “very good” (Genesis 1:31). It is essentially saying that God has made a mistake in creating a people with a certain complexion— that they somehow deserve lesser treatment though they are an imago dei, an image of God. We would not dare to insult the handiwork of a master craftsman, why would we do so to our creator?

Secondly, by dividing people and treating people differently based on the distinction of race, we are not understanding the message of the Gospel. I am talking about the Gospel that matters so profoundly that God would dedicate all sixty-six books of the Bible to explain it. I am talking about the Gospel that is so marvelous to explain that God sent His own Son to live a human life, to die a gruesome death, and to resurrect to eternal glory. Now, in the Bible, there are separations of ethnicity or ethnos (translated to “Gentiles” or “nations” in English), the differences in the governance, culture, history, and nation. Notice I said ethnicities, not races— we are of the one human race. Race is a mere social construct based on the complexion of our skin. This ethnic separation and distinction of the one race of mankind into many different ethnicities happened at Babel when God confused the language of man so that they would not further profane God by erecting a monument to themselves (Genesis 11:1-9). This separation was a punishment for sin but also a merciful deterrent to the end of mankind— it was God, after once having destroyed a world of sinners save for one family (Genesis 7:11-24), relenting from further annihilation of the sinful human race (which He very well deserved to do— Genesis 2:17) by dividing the nations so that He might prepare the one nation of Israel that would exemplify what He desired of all mankind.

This nation of Israel was our hope. Israel would go on to show what a covenant nation after God would resolve to look like and prepare the way for the Messiah who would save us from our own sins and bring the nations to Him (Isaiah 14:1). We see the propitiation for the sins of the elect of all nations on the cross when Jesus died (Matthew 27:45-54), and we see the coming together of those elect at the Pentecost (Acts 2:1-13,41). As we see in the account of the Pentecost, when the Holy Spirit came to dwell upon men, the disciples spoke in tongues of all nations— by the Holy Spirit, by the work of God Himself, nations of different tongue once separated at Babel are brought together under the one Priestly Kingship of Jesus Christ. Yet, we see this occurrence in that house in Jerusalem as not only a recovery of the state before Babel, but a redemption of the evil that has been turned for good— our God turns what men meant for evil to what is good (Genesis 50:20). It is a redemption, because if it were a mere recovery, all of the people in the room would have started speaking the same language. Furthermore, all of the people who could hear would have suddenly started understanding that language. No, rather, here, these people who are born of the Spirit (John 3:5-8) speak in different physical language, but with the one understanding of the Spirit that only those regenerated can hear. We see a redemption because within the Church, the different languages and ethnicities are redeemed from being signs of separation and division between peoples to the sign of Christ’s power to overcome the sins of men that caused division in the first place. When we hear the different languages and ways of worshipping the Lord in church, we ought to leap with joy as it is a foretaste of the things to come (Revelation 7:9). We see the power of the Gospel in firstly overcoming the sin that have made us to be enemies of God (Romans 5:10), and secondly in its power to bring disparate ethnicities to become brothers and sisters in Christ— the Jews (the covenant chosen nation of Israel) and all the different Gentiles (the ethnos, the rest of the world) have become reconciled through the work of God (Ephesians 2:11; Romans 11:17). Christ completes the greatest commandments that we were never able (and would have never been able) to complete: to love God and to love our neighbors (Deuteronomy 6:5; Leviticus 19:18; Matthew 22:36-40).

If a Christian truly believes this, then racism should be the farthest thing from his mind. If Christ closed the gap of enmity between the ethnicities, why should we further divide the Church into groups of a socially constructed, trivial, artificial distinction of the color of our skin? If Christ overcame the God-designed distinctions of nations, language, and culture, would he not be able to overcome the man-constructed distinction of race? If we know the goodness of the Gospel, even outside the Church, the Christian, in the evangelistic hopes of sharing the love of Christ that made right the relationship between both God and man, ought to show love to the world. Christ has brought all kinds of people together in Himself and in Christ— racism is defeated. It was Christ who defeated sin, including racism, because we, sinners, could not do it ourselves.


A Human Solution

Because it was Christ, not us, who defeated racism, and because He is our solution to the persistence of racism, I cannot help but bring up the concerns and genuine distress for what the world proposes as the human solution to racism. We have seen many different words and groups arise out of the chaos of the racism struggles, and among them are those who use the language of “white privilege”, “social justice” and “systemic racism”. I optimistically believe that most who use such language are not using them in their original intended definitions— many are merely misinformed and perhaps malinformed by those who do really know what they imply. However, if we understand what is called “critical theory” that supplies the foundation for concepts such as “white privilege”, “social justice” and “systemic racism”, we would find it more disconcerting to use such phrases freely and openly.

Now, just to introduce critical theory4 in several sentences is hard to do, however the core doctrines of critical theory are rather cogent. Finding its roots in Marxism from the Frankfurt School, critical theory assumes a class division of those in power— the oppressors, and those who are under their power— the oppressed, in society. Those that hold the political, economic, and social powers withhold rights and privileges to the oppressed while they themselves employ systemic means of holding onto power. There is a borrowed idea from classical Marxism that outlines a “class struggle” between the oppressed and the oppressors where there will come a time when the oppressed will overcome the oppressor by means of overthrowing the power of the oppressors and the systemic oppression that they have created. Critical theory is applied over social categorizations, and what this means is that the separation between the oppressors and the oppressed is the person’s intrinsic social category. The social category an individual is born into or attaches to is what determines whether or not that individual is an oppressor or an oppressed. Finally, the definition of oppression becomes defined once the classes are formed. An oppression is any form of action committed by the oppressors that the oppressed find to be offensive to them. Because the oppressors cannot understand the fullness of the oppressed experience, the oppressed holds the moral authority of the situation giving them the authority to decide what is right and wrong in an oppressor’s conduct.

Applying this to race, we have critical race theory. Critical race theory defines the black people as the oppressed and the white people as being the oppressors. White people both unknowingly and knowingly oppress the black population while the black people are oppressed by the system in place. Since the white person is born into their “whiteness” they have what is called “white privilege” that comes with it the privileges in that society for being of the lighter skin tone. On the other hand, since the black person is born into their darker complexion, they have been born into oppression with a “lived experience” and have also the moral right and authority to judge what is racist and what is not racist. Racism becomes what the oppressed deem to be oppressive, not the traditional definition of discriminating based on skin color— it becomes a systemic phenomena. Though there are conversations regarding other races, in the United States, critical race theory has primarily indulged itself in the two classes of white and black. Now, if you have read my distress towards racism and its incompatibility with the Gospel above, you may already see that critical theory is equivalently antithetical to the Gospel.

Critical race theory does not treat the problem of racism as a sin, but rather as a matter of a system. The concept of systemic racism asserts that the laws and practices of the political, social and economic institutions of the United States are inherently oppressive to the black population while of benefit to the white people. It is from this idea of systemic racism that the terms such as “white privilege” and “white fragility” are derived. White people are supposed to have certain privileges and benefits from a system that has been shaped in the hands of the white powerholders. Furthermore, they are inherently racist and are therefore fearful of discussions on race— “white fragility”. Such beliefs are problematic because it groups an entire people based on their skin color and assumes that anyone under that category is of a certain character and social position. This is by definition racist and hence the sin of partiality (James 2:7). If we are meaning to condemn the action of categorizing black people in a certain way, we must never do the same to any other race.

However, according to critical theory, this is a fine thing to do because racism is no longer a transgression against God, but it is what the oppressed believe to be right or wrong (as they are the moral authorities). Man has assumed the authority of God as Adam did in the Garden (Genesis 3:5-7). Under critical theory, racism is not an attack on an image bearer and hence an offense to the Creator, but it is what those of the darker complexion believe to be oppressive by the actions of the pale complexion. Racism is no longer a sin, but a systemic condition. Suddenly, the Creator is cast out of the business of His creation.


Systemic or Sin

This is the one of the biggest problems of critical race theory that is so profoundly perilous for any Christian to employ. By defining racism as a systemic issue, we have cast off God as the solution to the problem of racism. If racism is merely a systemic issue, then the solution is for mankind to find. No longer is Christ necessary for all reconciliation and all redemption, but Christ becomes merely a stepping stone to the main goal of systemic revival. As I explained above regarding the Pentecost, the work of loving our neighbors as ourselves was not something we could have ever done, and hence even that task Christ took on for us by loving us to the point of death. In loving us, Jesus was not merely being a moralistic teacher, but living out the perfect life that we could not as a perfect God-man to atone for our inability. Critical theory asserts that the solution to racism is revolution and resistance against the system, while Christianity proclaims that the solution is the redemption through Jesus Christ.

In Christianity, racism is not a systemic problem that we have to solve after Jesus has solved our sin problem. No, racism is a human sin that has been paid for by Jesus that we are to take advantage of by living out the reality of our salvation by not committing the sin of racism. Racism is solved by living out the Gospel reality. We know that even our sins of hating God were nailed to the cross with Jesus (Romans 5:10), why would we not understand the same with the sin of partiality regarding race (Colossians 2:14)? It is finished (John 19:30), and now our sanctification is the living out of that truth and fighting the old self that once loved to sin (2 Corinthians 5:17). The solution to racism is the Church that binds together nations and all peoples to be united under one King Jesus.

This improper view of racism as merely a systemic problem further exacerbates itself in that since sin is no longer the conscious action of an individual to act against another human being but the workings of the system, the fault lies with the entire group that is the oppressors within that said system. This makes racism impersonal and groups people based on skin color as guilty without a trial— it is unjust. The oppressors, the white people, are inherently oppressive because of the position they hold in the system. As those who are of a dominant position, they have both intentionally and unintentionally oppressed the black people. They are racist by birth. Furthermore, because the black people are the oppressed of the system, they have what is called the “lived experience” to determine what oppression is. This elevates man to the level of moral authority to decide what is right and what is wrong. This is quite counter to the Gospel narrative in that the Fall of mankind due to the fact that all man has sinned (Romans 3:23) is replaced with the rise of oppressors and the need for human salvation from God by God (Romans 5:9) is redacted with the addition of human revolution and class struggle against the oppressors. And since it is the system that creates oppression, in critical race theory, the concept of original sin is exchanged for the “white sin”, “white privilege”, and “white fragility” that is inherent in only the white people who are the architects of the oppressive system and not in all mankind. The core of our need for the Son is utterly demolished.

If I had not made it clear already, critical theory divides people based only on the color of their skin and gives them assigned roles in a system it creates. It assumes that people have certain sins and oppressive desires before even knowing them— all people are sinful and have a faulty mind, but to assume and judge a fellow human being without truly knowing is to be sinful by bearing false witness. It is by design racist in itself. Even if the entire world were to use its language and exalt its message, if we truly believe in the reconciliation to Christ that has been bought for us, and if we are truly ministers and messengers of that reconciliation (2 Corinthians 5:18-19), this is one theory that we should not imitate or even slightly resemble.


Fighting Injustice

We should most assuredly fight human injustices while we live on this earth that God has wonderfully and beautifully created. We are to fight against racist practices where they exist. If the court judges a black man differently from a white man, we must appeal for justice. Furthermore, if a law is passed that judges black and white people differently, we must fight against it. Yet, as we do so, we have to be careful in our intentions and our discernment of whether or not a said law or practice is racially related. That is, a law in itself has no personality except in its language. If a law is explicitly racist in its wording, we must protest it for being racist and hence not showing the purposes of God’s justice. However, if we are determining a practice to be racist retroactively, we must be careful that we are not once again focusing on the system when we ought to be focusing on the sin. We must understand that the person behind the law may well be racist, but laws themselves are not often racist unattached to a sinner. Fighting injustice for a Christian is an action of desire for God’s goodness to abound on earth, not an attempt to set up a new kingdom on earth that is to the glory of man as those at Babel had done.

Furthermore, we must remember that there are such things as racism. We must take care to not hold any prejudice against people. This is not a group process, but an individual process. Sin, as we understand it as Christians, starts from the heart and hence is not merely a systemic order. We need to daily kill the parts of us that seek to view those who look different as recipients of attack as such a way of viewing the world is to view the world with the image of ourselves as the standard and not God as the standard— it is to think that what we feel is better than what God has created. Let us see the Church as Christ views them— as His brothers and sisters, and let us view the people of the world as God views them— as His image bearers.


Justice in Christ

I believe that most of the Church who use the words of critical theory are merely not informed sufficiently of the actual implications and workings of the theory. Words such as “social justice” are often thrown around without much explanation as to what they mean and we are left to assume what they mean to convey. Some often do not know that “social justice” does not mean “justice in society”, but “justice in terms of the distribution of wealth, opportunities, and privileges within a society”5. Some do not know that it is derived from and has implications of the much more sinister critical theory that perhaps we have not been informed as much about6. I genuinely hope that this essay has shed some light on the need for us to be knowledgeable about the implications that we are enforcing when we use certain words and phrases to support justice.

Furthermore, I hope that if any had held such unbiblical views of implementing justice in the world, that they would reconsider whether or not the causes and the methods they are employing in attempting to create a better society here on earth is replicating the mistakes of those at Babel by erecting a monument of a human city to ourselves whereas we should be seeking the city that is to come (Hebrews 13:14). Even in our fight against racism, we must look different from the world— we cannot protest in the same way, instigate disruptions the same way, and we cannot believe in homogeneity to what the world believes. We must be deftly informed of the fast-paced culture of the world and must be able to see it in the lens of Scriptures. In discernment, we must denounce and condemn un-Godly theories publicly and learn to distinguish the world from the faith. We should not be quoting and promoting literatures that build off of critical theory which drive further a worldview far apart from the truth. We should not be calling out the white people to “repent of white sins and privileges”, but should be uniting together to fight racism. This act of division defines racism in the world’s perspective and attempts to solve it using a worldly solution that is racist by the Biblical definition. We should not group an entire skin color into one collective “black experience” or “white experience” that robs them of their humanity, but rather see that we have a God who is creative enough to design each individual in their own special manner— one book about the “lived experience” about an entire race trivializes a person to a caricatured figment of construction. Our definition of justice for racism needs not any other basis than Scripture itself.

We are driven to fight racism not just because of its effects on mankind, but primarily because it is sinful against a Holy God. We cannot endure racism because it is an insult to God, and if so, we need to make sure that our protest and fight for justice does not look like the world. We should not be turning to yet another evil (critical theory) to seek to repair another evil (racism). If our definition of justice at any point in time is the progressive movement of humanity rather than the exercise and judgement of the Laws of God upon the people, we may be building a human kingdom rather than a heavenly one. If the world observes your protest and fight and sees it to be no different from theirs, it is perplexing because that means that you are no longer sharing Gospel truth to expand the Kingdom of God but rather merely exercising power to expand the kingdom of man. Even our desire for a better society ought to be borne out of a desire to foreshadow the New Heaven and the New Earth that is to come through Christ, not because we want to bring a new earth to this current one.

Let us not forget that it is God’s mercy that allows us to live despite the fact that we are sinners. God does not need to supply us with His goodness. We do not deserve a perfect world, but actually punishment. We confuse justice with mercy often— justice says that we deserve eternal punishment for our sins, mercy says that we can live to worship and glorify God despite our sins because of Jesus. It is a privilege that we are allowed to even breathe and live, and the Christians have the greatest privilege of being called the righteous sons and daughters of God who have the ability to do righteous things that the world cannot do. If so, let us define justice Biblically, not by critical theory or any sociological concept. The greatest injustice was our rebellion towards God— the creation rejecting the creator, and hence that is why we must always preach the Gospel. If truly we believe that racism is a sin and an affront to God, then why do we not treat all other sins with such violent passions? It seems that we are ambivalent to graver sins. Should we not protest the disgusting nature of pornography and sex industries with the same degree of hatred? Should we not hate abortion— which is infanticide— with a greater lament? It seems that we are more concerned about the plight of man rather than the holiness God requires. If the solution to such sins is Christ and through Him, the Church, the solution also to racism is Christ and the Church. The solution to all sin is always Christ, and Christ alone. Our greatest form of protest against injustice including racism is through our preaching of the Gospel because our injustice was primarily towards God, and secondly to His images. Only Christ can mend what we have broken.

Let us be ever constantly informed about the language that the world uses and not appropriate it for Christian usage because it is what the populous desires. We should most unquestionably challenge injustices in the public and private settings, but we must do it with God’s definition of justice in mind and with God’s response in mind. God’s response is always accompanied by the Gospel truth.


Conclusion

We, as Christians, must indeed emphasize and exemplify the justice that God so loves. But first, to do so, let us seek to remember what it means to stand before the judgement of God. If any of us, any member of the human race, were to stand judgement before the Holy Holy Holy Lord (Isaiah 6:3), we would deserve the ultimate punishment of death— an utter and eternal separation from the love of God. However, through the grace of God, for the elect, the Church, a propitiation has been made through the substitutionary atonement of Christ in our stead. God loved justice so much to the point of loving and dying for sinners. With such otherworldly truth in our minds, we, Christians, must comprehend justice to be something required by God, but also upheld by God. We, as sinful human beings cannot be the catalysts for perfect justice on earth, and certain cannot facilitate it by human means. However, for the Church, Christ has accomplished what we could not, and He is not only the alpha to the solution, but the omega to the solution. He solved the problem of sin to reconcile us to Himself, and with the rest of His beloved Bride— the Christian Church. We, the Church, who know such truth, must live out that truth partly by embodying justice within ourselves in conduct and in our support of justice in our governments, but more importantly, by defending the faith from worldly influences and sharing the Gospel that will bring those once far apart in ethnicity and nations to the one Kingdom of God (Matthew 28:19).
